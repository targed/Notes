### **2.5 P2P Applications: File Distribution**
  
  This section explores the peer-to-peer (P2P) architecture, contrasting it with the traditional client-server model, and delves into the most popular P2P file distribution protocol, BitTorrent.
#### **Review of the P2P Architecture**
  
  *   **No Always-On Server:** The defining characteristic is the absence of a central, always-on server. Communication happens directly between end systems.
  *   **Dual Roles:** Hosts, called **peers**, act as both clients (requesting data) and servers (providing data).
  *   **Self-Scalability:** This is the key advantage. As new peers join to demand service, they also bring new service capacity (their upload bandwidth), allowing the system to scale gracefully.
  *   **Challenges:** The architecture is more complex to manage due to the intermittent connectivity of peers and their dynamic IP addresses. This intermittent participation is called **churn**.
#### **File Distribution: Client-Server vs. P2P**

  Let's analyze the time it takes to distribute a large file of size $F$ from one source to $N$ peers using both models. This highlights the scalability advantage of P2P.
  
  **Variables:**
  *   $F$: Size of the file (in bits).
  *   $N$: Number of peers wanting to download the file.
  *   $u_s$: Upload capacity of the origin server.
  *   $u_i$: Upload capacity of peer $i$.
  *   $d_i$: Download capacity of peer $i$.
  
  **1. Client-Server File Distribution**
  In this model, the origin server must send a complete copy of the file to each of the $N$ clients.
  
  *   **Server Transmission Bottleneck:** The server has to sequentially upload $N$ copies of the file. The total time required for this is:
  \(\text{Time}_{\text{server}} = \frac{N \cdot F}{u_s}\)
  *   **Client Download Bottleneck:** The slowest client determines the minimum time for everyone to finish. Let $d_{min}$ be the download rate of the slowest client. The time for this client to download the file is:
    \(\text{Time}_{\text{client}} = \frac{F}{d_{min}}\)
  
  The total time to distribute the file, $D_{c-s}$, is limited by whichever of these two is larger.
  
  **Equation for Client-Server Distribution Time:**
  \(D_{c-s} \ge \max \left( \frac{N \cdot F}{u_s}, \frac{F}{d_{min}} \right)\)
  
  **Key Observation:** As the number of peers, $N$, increases, the time to distribute the file, $\frac{N \cdot F}{u_s}$, grows **linearly**. This model does not scale well.
  
  **2. Peer-to-Peer (P2P) File Distribution**
  In P2P, peers help distribute the file to each other.
  
  *   **Server Transmission:** The server only needs to upload *at least one* complete copy of the file into the P2P network. The time for this is: $\frac{F}{u_s}$.
  *   **Client Download:** The slowest client still needs at least $\frac{F}{d_{min}}$ time to download its copy.
  *   **Aggregate Upload Bottleneck:** The crucial difference is that the total upload capacity of the system is the sum of the server's upload rate *plus* the upload rates of all the peers. The total amount of data to be distributed is $N \cdot F$.
  \(\text{Time}_{\text{aggregate}} = \frac{N \cdot F}{u_s + \sum_{i=1}^{N} u_i}\)
  
  The total distribution time is the maximum of these three factors.
  
  **Equation for P2P Distribution Time:**
  \(D_{P2P} \ge \max \left( \frac{F}{u_s}, \frac{F}{d_{min}}, \frac{N \cdot F}{u_s + \sum u_i} \right)\)
  
  **Key Observation:** As $N$ increases, the term $\frac{N \cdot F}{\sum u_i}$ (the peer upload contribution) also increases. The overall distribution time does not grow linearly but flattens out, demonstrating the **self-scalability** of P2P systems.
#### **BitTorrent: A Practical P2P Protocol**
  
  BitTorrent is the most popular P2P protocol for file distribution. It uses a clever incentive mechanism to encourage peers to upload while they download.
  
  *   **Key Terminology:**
    *   **Torrent:** The group of all peers actively exchanging chunks of a specific file.
    *   **Chunks:** The file is broken down into fixed-size pieces, typically **256 Kilobytes**. Peers download and upload these individual chunks.
    *   **Tracker:** A central server that *tracks* which peers are in a given torrent. **Note:** BitTorrent is not a *pure* P2P system because it relies on this tracker. If the tracker goes down, new peers cannot join the torrent. (Modern "trackerless" systems use a distributed hash table instead).
  
  *   **How a Peer Joins a Torrent:**
    1.  A new peer (let's call her Alice) first contacts the **tracker** to get a list of all other peers currently in the torrent.
    2.  The tracker provides a list of IP addresses.
    3.  Alice then establishes TCP connections to a subset of these peers, becoming their **"neighbor."**
  
  *   **Exchanging Chunks:**
    *   **Requesting Chunks:**
        *   Periodically, Alice asks her neighbors for the list of chunks they have.
        *   Alice then requests chunks that she is missing. She prioritizes requesting the **rarest chunks first**. This strategy ensures that all chunks remain available in the torrent, preventing a situation where the last few chunks are held by only one peer who might leave.
    *   **Sending Chunks (The "Tit-for-Tat" Mechanism):**
        *   To decide whom to upload to, Alice prioritizes the peers who are currently sending her chunks at the highest rate.
        *   Specifically, Alice identifies her **top four fastest providers** and continuously sends them chunks. All other neighbors are **"choked,"** meaning they do not receive chunks from her.
        *   This list of the top four is re-evaluated every 10 seconds.
    *   **Optimistic Unchoking:** To allow new peers to get started, every 30 seconds, Alice picks one *additional* peer at random and starts sending them chunks. This is called **optimistic unchoking**.
        *   This gives the new peer a chance to receive data and, in return, potentially become one of Alice's top four providers, creating a mutually beneficial relationship.
  
  **Summary of Tit-for-Tat:** This mechanism provides a powerful incentive for peers to contribute (upload). The faster a peer uploads to you, the faster you will upload to them, resulting in faster download speeds for everyone who cooperates.
