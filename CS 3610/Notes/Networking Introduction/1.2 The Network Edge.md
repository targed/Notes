### **1.2 The Network Edge**
  
  The network edge consists of the hosts (end systems) that we use, as well as the access networks and physical media that connect them to the rest of the Internet.
#### **Access Networks and Physical Media**
  
  The fundamental question this section addresses is: How do end systems connect to the first router (the "edge router") on the path to the global Internet?
  
  When evaluating different access networks, two key characteristics are important:
  
  *   **Transmission Rate:** The speed of the network, measured in bits per second (bps).
  *   **Shared vs. Dedicated Access:** Whether the connection link is shared among multiple users or dedicated to a single user.
  
  There are three main categories of access networks:
  
  1.  **Residential Access Networks:** Connect homes to the internet.
  2.  **Institutional Access Networks:** Used in companies, schools, and universities.
  3.  **Mobile Access Networks:** Connect mobile devices.
#### **Types of Access Networks**
  
  **1. Residential Access: Cable Internet**
  *   **Technology:** Uses the existing cable television infrastructure. This is a **shared medium**.
  *   **Infrastructure:** A **Hybrid Fiber Coax (HFC)** network is used, where fiber optic cables connect the cable headend to neighborhood-level junctions, and from there, coaxial cables run to individual houses.
  *   **Mechanism:** It uses **Frequency Division Multiplexing (FDM)**, which transmits different signals (e.g., for data, TV) on different frequency bands over the same cable.
  *   **Performance:**
    *   Connections are typically **asymmetric**, meaning the downstream (to the home) speed is much faster than the upstream (from the home) speed.
    *   **Downstream rates:** Can range from 40 Megabits per second (Mbps) to over 1.2 Gigabits per second (Gbps).
    *   **Upstream rates:** Typically range from 30 to 100 Mbps.
    *   Since it's a shared network, the available bandwidth is divided among the active users in the neighborhood.
  
  **2. Residential Access: Digital Subscriber Line (DSL)**
  *   **Technology:** Uses existing telephone lines to transfer digital data.
  *   **Infrastructure:** The line from the home connects directly to the telephone company's local **central office (CO)**. The DSLAM (Digital Subscriber Line Access Multiplexer) at the CO separates data and phone signals.
  *   **Mechanism:** Like cable, it uses different frequencies for data and voice, allowing you to use the internet and make phone calls simultaneously.
  *   **Performance:**
    *   It is a **dedicated** connection from the home to the central office, not shared with neighbors.
    *   Connections are also **asymmetric**.
    *   **Downstream rates:** Typically 24-52 Mbps.
    *   **Upstream rates:** Typically 3.5-16 Mbps.
    *   The maximum rate is highly dependent on the distance from the home to the central office; performance degrades significantly over longer distances.
  
  **3. Home Networks**
  *   A typical home network connects a cable or DSL modem to a router.
  *   This router then provides connectivity to various devices in the home through:
    *   **Wired Ethernet:** Offering speeds of 1 Gbps or more.
    *   **Wireless (Wi-Fi):** The router acts as a wireless access point, with common speeds of 54 Mbps to 450 Mbps and beyond, depending on the Wi-Fi standard (e.g., 802.11b/g/n).
  *   Often, the modem, router, and Wi-Fi access point are integrated into a single device.
  
  **4. Enterprise and Institutional Networks**
  *   These are used by large organizations like companies and universities.
  *   They consist of a mix of switches and routers to handle a large number of devices.
  *   **Ethernet** is commonly used for wired access, with speeds of 100 Mbps, 1 Gbps, or 10 Gbps.
  *   **Wi-Fi** provides wireless access, with speeds similar to home networks.
  
  **5. Data Center Networks**
  *   These are specialized networks that connect a massive number of servers together and to the broader Internet.
  *   They utilize high-bandwidth links capable of carrying 10s to 100s of Gbps.
  
  **6. Wireless Access Networks**
  *   These connect end systems to a router wirelessly via a **base station** or **access point**.
  *   **Wireless Local Area Networks (WLANs):**
    *   The most common example is **Wi-Fi**, based on the IEEE 802.11 family of standards.
    *   Typically used within a building, with a range of up to 100 feet.
    *   Transmission rates vary from 11 Mbps (802.11b) to 450 Mbps (802.11n) and higher with newer standards.
  *   **Wide-Area Cellular Access Networks:**
    *   Provided by mobile network operators (e.g., Verizon, AT&T).
    *   Covers large geographical areas (tens of kilometers).
    *   Technologies include 4G and the emerging 5G, offering speeds in the tens of Mbps.
#### **Physical Media**
  
  Physical media are the actual links used to transmit signals. They can be classified into two types:
  
  *   **Guided Media:** Signals are guided along a solid medium.
    *   **Twisted-Pair Copper Wire:** Used for DSL and Ethernet.
    *   **Coaxial Cable:** Used for cable internet.
    *   **Fiber Optic Cable:** Carries light pulses and offers very high speeds (10s-100s of Gbps) with low error rates.
  *   **Unguided Media:** Signals propagate freely through space.
    *   **Wireless Radio:** Signals are carried in the electromagnetic spectrum. It is a broadcast medium and is susceptible to interference and obstruction.
#### **Host Sending Function and Transmission Delay**
  
  When a host sends data, it performs the following steps:
  1.  Takes a large application message (e.g., a file).
  2.  Breaks it into smaller chunks called **packets**.
  3.  Transmits these packets one by one into the access network link.
  
  The time it takes to push all the bits of a packet onto the link is the **packet transmission delay**.
  
  **Equation for Packet Transmission Delay:**
  
  If a packet has a length of **L** bits and the link's transmission rate is **R** bits per second, the time to transmit the packet is:
  
  **Transmission Delay = L / R**
