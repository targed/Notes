### **1.3 The Network Core**
  
  The network core is the mesh of interconnected routers that forms the backbone of the internet. Its primary job is to move data from a source end system to a destination end system.
#### **Packet Switching**
  
  The internet's core operates on the principle of **packet switching**.
  
  1.  **Packetization:** End systems (hosts) break large application-layer messages (e.g., a web page, an email) into smaller chunks of data called **packets**.
  2.  **Forwarding:** These packets are then sent into the network. Each router in the path receives the full packet, inspects its destination address, and forwards it to the next router along the path. This continues until the packet reaches its final destination.
#### **Two Key Functions of the Network Core**
  
  There are two fundamental functions that enable packet switching: **forwarding** and **routing**.
  
  1.  **Forwarding (or Switching)**
    *   **Definition:** The *local* action of a router moving an arriving packet from one of its input links to the appropriate output link.
    *   **Mechanism:** When a packet arrives, the router examines the packet's header to find its destination address. It then uses a **local forwarding table** to determine which output link the packet should be sent on. Each router in the internet has its own forwarding table.
  
  2.  **Routing**
    *   **Definition:** The *global* action of determining the end-to-end path that packets take from the source to the destination across the entire network.
    *   **Mechanism:** **Routing algorithms** are used to calculate these paths. The collective result of these routing algorithms across the network is what determines the contents of the individual forwarding tables inside each router.
  
  **Analogy: A Cross-Country Car Trip**
  *   **Routing** is like planning your entire trip from San Jose, California to Northampton, Massachusetts. You use a map (or GPS) to determine the complete sequence of roads and highways you will take. This is a global, end-to-end process.
  *   **Forwarding** is like arriving at a single highway interchange (a router). You look at the road signs (the forwarding table) to decide which specific exit to take to continue on your pre-planned route. This is a local action at that specific interchange.
#### **Packet Switching: Store-and-Forward**
  
  Packet-switched networks use a mechanism called **store-and-forward**.
  
  *   **Definition:** A router must receive the *entire* packet before it can begin to transmit the first bit of that packet onto the next outbound link. The packet is first stored in memory (in a buffer) and then forwarded.
  *   **Transmission Delay:** The time required to push all the bits of a packet onto a link.
  
  **Equation for Transmission Delay:**
  If a packet has a length of $L$ bits and the link's transmission rate is $R$ bits/second, the transmission delay is:
  
  $D_{trans} = \frac{L}{R}$
  
  For example, to transmit a 10 Kilobit packet on a 100 Mbps link, the transmission delay is (10,000 bits) / (100,000,000 bits/sec) = 0.1 milliseconds.
#### **Packet Queueing and Loss**
  
  *   **Queueing:** For each output link, a router has a buffer (or **queue**). If packets arrive at a router faster than they can be transmitted over the outbound link, they must wait in this queue. This is similar to cars waiting at a toll booth.
  *   **Packet Loss:** These queues have a finite memory capacity. If a packet arrives and the queue for its outbound link is already full, the router has no place to store it. The router will **drop** the packet, and it is considered **lost**. This is a major source of problems that network protocols must handle.
#### **Alternative to Packet Switching: Circuit Switching**
  
  Before packet switching, networks (like the traditional telephone network) used **circuit switching**.
  
  *   **Core Idea:** End-to-end resources along a path (buffers, link transmission rate) are reserved for the duration of a "call" or connection.
  *   **Mechanism:**
    1.  A dedicated end-to-end "circuit" is established before data can flow.
    2.  Data can then flow with a guaranteed, constant transmission rate.
    3.  After the session, the circuit is torn down, and the resources are freed.
  *   **Inefficiency:** If the reserved circuit is not being used (e.g., during periods of silence in a phone call), the resources remain allocated and go to waste. They cannot be used by other connections.
#### **Multiplexing in Circuit-Switched Networks**
  
  To share a link's resources among multiple circuits, multiplexing techniques are used:
  
  *   **Frequency Division Multiplexing (FDM):** The frequency spectrum of a link is divided into narrow frequency bands. Each call is allocated its own dedicated band for the duration of the call.
  *   **Time Division Multiplexing (TDM):** Time is divided into repeating frames, and each frame is divided into a fixed number of time slots. Each call is allocated one specific time slot in every frame, giving it a periodic opportunity to transmit.
#### **Comparing Packet Switching and Circuit Switching**
  
  Packet switching is far more efficient for the "bursty" nature of typical internet data, where users are not constantly sending information.
  
  *   **Example Scenario:**
    *   A 1 Gbps link is shared by multiple users.
    *   When a user is active, they need 100 Mbps.
    *   Each user is only active 10% of the time.
  
  *   **Circuit Switching:** Each user requires a dedicated 100 Mbps circuit. Therefore, the 1 Gbps link can support only $1000 \text{ Mbps} / 100 \text{ Mbps/user} = 10$ users.
  
  *   **Packet Switching:** Since users are idle 90% of the time, the link can be shared. With 35 users, for example, the probability of more than 10 of them being active at the exact same time is extremely low (less than 0.0004). Packet switching takes advantage of this statistical property, allowing many more users to share the same link. This is known as **statistical multiplexing gain**.
  
  **Conclusion:** Packet switching allows for much better sharing of network resources but comes at the cost of potential queueing delays and packet loss. Circuit switching provides guaranteed performance but can be very wasteful of resources.
#### **Structure of the Internet: A "Network of Networks"**
  
  The internet is not a single, monolithic network but a complex, interconnected hierarchy of networks managed by different organizations.
  
  *   At the "center" of the internet is a small number of large, well-connected networks. These include:
    *   **Tier-1 ISPs:** These are major commercial ISPs with national and international reach (e.g., AT&T, Sprint, NTT).
    *   **Content Provider Networks:** Large companies like Google and Facebook operate their own massive private networks to connect their data centers to the internet, often bypassing Tier-1 ISPs to get closer to users.
  *   These central networks connect to **Regional ISPs**.
  *   Regional ISPs, in turn, connect to the local **Access ISPs** that provide service to homes and businesses.
  *   **Internet Exchange Points (IXPs)** are physical locations where different ISPs can connect to exchange traffic with each other directly, in an arrangement called **peering**.
