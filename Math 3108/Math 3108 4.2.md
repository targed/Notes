## Section 4.2: Null Spaces, Column Spaces, Row Spaces, and Linear Transformations
### Null Spaces
  
  The **null space** of an $m \times n$ matrix $A$ is the set of all solutions of the homogeneous equation $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$.
  
  $\text{Nul } A = \{\mathbf{x}: \mathbf{x} \text{ is in } \mathbb{R}^n \text{ and } A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}\}$
  
  Equivalently, $\text{Nul } A$ is the set of all $\mathbf{x}$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$ that are mapped into the zero vector of $\mathbb{R}^m$ via the linear transformation $\mathbf{x} \mapsto A\mathbf{x}$.
### Example 1
  
  Show that $\text{Nul } A$ is a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^n$.
  
  **Solution:**
  
  Let $A$ be an $m \times n$ matrix. We need to verify the three properties of a subspace:
  
  a. **Zero vector:**  The zero vector $\mathbf{0}$ (in $\mathbb{R}^n$) is in $\text{Nul } A$ because $A\mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0}$ (where the $\mathbf{0}$ on the right is in $\mathbb{R}^m$).
  
  b. **Closure under addition:** Let $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ be in $\text{Nul } A$.  This means $A\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{0}$ and $A\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$.  We need to show that $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}$ is also in $\text{Nul } A$.  We have:
  
    $A(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) = A\mathbf{u} + A\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0} + \mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0}$.
  
    Therefore, $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}$ is in $\text{Nul } A$.
  
  c. **Closure under scalar multiplication:** Let $\mathbf{u}$ be in $\text{Nul } A$, and let $c$ be a scalar.  This means $A\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{0}$. We need to show that $c\mathbf{u}$ is also in $\text{Nul } A$. We have:
  
    $A(c\mathbf{u}) = c(A\mathbf{u}) = c\mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0}$.
  
    Therefore, $c\mathbf{u}$ is in $\text{Nul } A$.
  
  Since all three properties hold, $\text{Nul } A$ is a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^n$.
### Theorem
  
  The null space of an $m \times n$ matrix $A$ is a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^n$.
  
  Equivalently, the set of all solutions to a system $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$ of $m$ homogeneous linear equations in $n$ unknowns is a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^n$.
### Example 2
  
  Determine if $\mathbf{w} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ -3 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$ is in $\text{Nul } A$, where $A = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 21 & 19 \\ 13 & 23 & 2 \\ 8 & 14 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$.
  
  **Solution:**
  
  To determine if $\mathbf{w}$ is in $\text{Nul } A$, we compute $A\mathbf{w}$:
  
  $A\mathbf{w} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 21 & 19 \\ 13 & 23 & 2 \\ 8 & 14 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ -3 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} (5)(5) + (21)(-3) + (19)(2) \\ (13)(5) + (23)(-3) + (2)(2) \\ (8)(5) + (14)(-3) + (1)(2) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 25 - 63 + 38 \\ 65 - 69 + 4 \\ 40 - 42 + 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$
  
  Since $A\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{0}$, $\mathbf{w}$ is in $\text{Nul } A$.
### Example 3
  
  Determine whether $W = \left\{ \begin{bmatrix} a \\ b \\ c \\ d \end{bmatrix} : \begin{array}{l} a + 3b = c \\ b + c + a = d \end{array} \right\}$ is a vector space.
  
  **Solution:**
  
  We can rewrite the conditions defining $W$ as homogeneous linear equations:
  
  $a + 3b - c = 0$
  $a + b + c - d = 0$
  
  This can be written in matrix form as:
  
  $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 & -1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 & 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} a \\ b \\ c \\ d \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$
  
  Let $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 & -1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 & 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$ and $\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} a \\ b \\ c \\ d \end{bmatrix}$. Then $W$ is the set of all $\mathbf{x}$ such that $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$.  Therefore, $W = \text{Nul } A$. Since the null space of a matrix is always a vector space (a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^n$), $W$ is a vector space.
### Explicit vs. Implicit Descriptions of Nul A
  
  *   When a vector space $V$ is described as the null space of a matrix $A$, we have an **implicit description** of $V$ since $V$ is defined by a condition that must be checked.
  *   To find an **explicit description** of $\text{Nul } A$, we simply solve the system to find a set of vectors which span the space.
### Example 4
  
  Find an explicit description of $\text{Nul } A$ by listing vectors which span the null space.
  
  $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 5 & -4 & -3 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & -2 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$
  
  **Solution:**
  First, solve Ax = 0
  $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 5 & -4 & -3 & 1 & 0\\ 0 & 1 & -2 & 1 & 0 & 0\\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}$
  
  $R_1 = R_1 - 5R_2$
  $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 6 & -8 & 1 & 0\\ 0 & 1 & -2 & 1 & 0 & 0\\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}$
  
  This gives:
  $x_1 = -6x_3 + 8x_4 - x_5$
  $x_2 = 2x_3 - x_4$
  $x_3 = x_3$
  $x_4 = x_4$
  $x_5 = x_5$
  
  Or:
  $\begin{bmatrix} -6x_3 + 8x_4 - x_5 \\ 2x_3 - x_4 \\ x_3 \\ x_4 \\ x_5 \end{bmatrix} = x_3\begin{bmatrix} -6 \\ 2 \\ 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + x_4\begin{bmatrix} 8 \\ -1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + x_5\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$
  
  Therefore the Null Space is spanned by the vectors:
  $\begin{bmatrix} -6 \\ 2 \\ 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 8 \\ -1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$
### Comments About Null Spaces
  
  *   The spanning set for $\text{Nul } A$ produced by our standard solution technique is automatically linearly independent.
  *   When $\text{Nul } A$ contains nonzero vectors, the number of vectors in the spanning set for $\text{Nul } A$ equals the number of free variables in the equation $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$.
### Column Spaces
  
  The **column space** of an $m \times n$ matrix $A$ is the set of all linear combinations of the columns of $A$.
  
  If $A = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{a}_1 & \mathbf{a}_2 & ... & \mathbf{a}_n \end{bmatrix}$, then $\text{Col } A = \text{Span}\{\mathbf{a}_1, \mathbf{a}_2, ..., \mathbf{a}_n\}$.
  
  $\text{Col } A$ is a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^m$.
### Example 5
  
  Show that $W = \left\{ \begin{bmatrix} b - c \\ 2b + c + d \\ 5c - 4d \\ d \end{bmatrix} : b, c, d \text{ real} \right\}$ is a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^4$ by showing that $W = \text{Col } A$ for some matrix $A$.
  
  **Solution:**
  
  We can rewrite a vector in $W$ as a linear combination:
  
  $\begin{bmatrix} b - c \\ 2b + c + d \\ 5c - 4d \\ d \end{bmatrix} = b\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + c\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \\ 5 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + d\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ -4 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$
  
  This shows that $W$ is the span of the vectors $\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$, $\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \\ 5 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$, and $\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ -4 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$.
  
  Therefore, if we let $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 5 & -4 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$, then $W = \text{Col } A$. Since the column space is always a subspace, $W$ is a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^4$.
### Comments About Column Spaces
  
  *   The column space of an $m \times n$ matrix $A$ is all of $\mathbb{R}^m$ if and only if the equation $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ has a solution for each $\mathbf{b}$ in $\mathbb{R}^m$.
  *   $\text{Col } A$ is the range of the linear transformation $\mathbf{x} \mapsto A\mathbf{x}$.
### The Row Space of a Matrix
  
  The **row space** of an $m \times n$ matrix $A$ is the set of all linear combinations of the rows of $A$, where each row of $A$ can be viewed as a vector in $\mathbb{R}^n$.
  
  Note that $\text{Row } A = \text{Col } A^T$.
  
  We generally write row vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$ horizontally as ordered $n$-tuples.
### Example 6
  
  Find a nonzero vector in $\text{Nul } A$, a nonzero vector in $\text{Col } A$, and a nonzero vector in $\text{Row } A$.
  
  $A = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 5 & -2 & 6 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 & 0 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$
  
  **Solution:**
  
  *   **Nul A:** To find a nonzero vector in $\text{Nul } A$, we need to find a nontrivial solution to $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$.  Row reducing $A$ helps:
  
  $A = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 5 & -2 & 6 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 & 0 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \sim \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 4 & 5 & -2 & 6 & 0\end{bmatrix} \sim \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & -2 & 2 & 0\end{bmatrix} \sim \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 2 & -1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & -2 & 2 & 0\end{bmatrix}$.
  
  $x_1 = -2x_3 + x_4$
  $x_2 = 2x_3 - 2x_4$
  $x_3 = x_3$
  $x_4 = x_4$
  
  $\mathbf{x} = x_3\begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 2 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + x_4 \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$.
  Let x3 and x4 each equal 1.
  We get $\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$ for a particular nonzero vector,
  
  *   **Col A:** Any column of $A$ is in $\text{Col } A$.  So, we can take the first column: $\begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$.
  
  *   **Row A:** Any row of $A$ is in $\text{Row } A$.  So, we can take the first row: $\begin{bmatrix} 4 & 5 & -2 & 6 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$.
### Contrasting Nul A and Col A where A is m x n
  
  | $\text{Nul } A$                                                                               | $\text{Col } A$                                                                           |
  | :------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | :---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
  | Is a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^n$                                                              | Is a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^m$                                                             |
  | Implicitly defined                                                                           | Explicitly defined                                                                       |
  | It takes time to find vectors in $\text{Nul } A$                                             | It is very easy to find vectors in $\text{Col } A$                                         |
  | There is no obvious relationship between entries in $A$ and $\text{Nul } A$                  | There is a very obvious relationship between entries in $A$ and $\text{Col } A$            |
  | A typical vector $\mathbf{v}$ in $\text{Nul } A$ has the property that $A\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$ | A typical vector $\mathbf{v}$ in $\text{Col } A$ has the property that $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{v}$ is consistent. |
  | Given a specific vector $\mathbf{v}$, it is easy to tell if $\mathbf{v}$ is in $\text{Nul } A$. Just compute $A\mathbf{v}$. | Given a specific vector $\mathbf{v}$, it may take effort to tell if $\mathbf{v}$ is in $\text{Col } A$ since row reduction of $\begin{bmatrix} A & \mathbf{v} \end{bmatrix}$ is needed. |
  | $\text{Nul } A = \{\mathbf{0}\}$ if and only if $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$ has only the trivial solution. | $\text{Col } A = \mathbb{R}^m$ if and only if $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ has a solution for every $\mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^m$. |
  | $\text{Nul } A = \{\mathbf{0}\}$ if and only if the linear transformation $\mathbf{x} \mapsto A\mathbf{x}$ is one-to-one. | $\text{Col } A = \mathbb{R}^m$ if and only if the linear transformation $\mathbf{x} \mapsto A\mathbf{x}$ maps $\mathbb{R}^n$ onto $\mathbb{R}^m$. |
### Linear Transformations
  
  A **linear transformation** $T$ from a vector space $V$ into a vector space $W$ is a rule that assigns to each vector $\mathbf{x}$ in $V$ a unique vector $T(\mathbf{x})$ in $W$ such that:
  
  i. $T(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) = T(\mathbf{u}) + T(\mathbf{v})$ for all $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}$ in $V$, and
  ii. $T(c\mathbf{u}) = cT(\mathbf{u})$ for all $\mathbf{u}$ in $V$ and all scalars $c$.
### Linear Transformations (Continued)
  
  If $T: V \rightarrow W$ is a linear transformation, then:
  
  *   The **kernel** of $T$ is the set of all $\mathbf{u}$ in $V$ such that $T(\mathbf{u}) = \mathbf{0}$.
  *   The **range** of $T$ is the set of all vectors in $W$ of the form $T(\mathbf{x})$ for some $\mathbf{x}$ in $V$.
  
  If $T$ is a matrix transformation $T(\mathbf{x}) = A\mathbf{x}$, then the kernel of $T$ is the null space of $A$ and the range of $T$ is the column space of $A$.
### Example 7
  
  Define $T: \mathbb{P}_2 \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^2$ by $T(\mathbf{p}) = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{p}(0) \\ \mathbf{p}(1) \end{bmatrix}$.
  
  a. If $\mathbf{p}(t) = 3 + 5t + 7t^2$, find $T(\mathbf{p})$.
  b. Show that $T$ is linear.
  c. Find a polynomial $\mathbf{p}$ in $\mathbb{P}_2$ that spans the kernel of $T$ and describe the range of $T$.
  
  **Solution:**
  a. $T(\mathbf{p}) =  \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{p}(0) \\ \mathbf{p}(1) \end{bmatrix}$
  $\mathbf{p}(0) = 3 + 5(0) + 7(0^2) = 3$
  $\mathbf{p}(1) = 3 + 5(1) + 7(1^2) = 15$
  
  $T(\mathbf{p}) = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 15\end{bmatrix}$
  
  b. We need to check the properties for linearity:
  $T(p+q) = T(p) + T(q)$
  and
  $T(cp) = cT(p)$
  
  $T(p+q) = \begin{bmatrix} p(0) + q(0) \\ p(1) + q(1)\end{bmatrix} =  \begin{bmatrix} p(0) \\ p(1)\end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} q(0) \\ q(1)\end{bmatrix} = T(p) + T(q)$
  
  $T(cp) =  \begin{bmatrix} cp(0) \\ cp(1)\end{bmatrix} = c\begin{bmatrix} p(0) \\ p(1)\end{bmatrix} = cT(p)$
  This function is linear because it follows those two rules.
  
  c. The kernel are all p that satisfy:
  $T(p) = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$
  $p(0) = 0$
  $p(1) = 0$
  $p(t) = at^2+bt+c$
  $p(0) = c = 0$
  $p(1) = a + b = 0$
  $a = -b$
  
  Therefore all p where $p(t) = at(t-1)$ will satisfy T(p) = 0.
  Any multiple of the polynomial t(t-1) is in the kernel, therefore the kernel is spanned by {t(t-1)}.
  
  The range of T(p) is any real number, because by altering a, b, and c of p(t), you can create any matrix of real numbers.
