## Section 7.1: Diagonalization of Symmetric Matrices
### Symmetric Matrices
  
  A matrix $A$ is **symmetric** if and only if $A^T = A$.
### Example 1
  
  Show that if $A$ is symmetric then $A^2$ is symmetric.
  
  **Solution:**
  
  We are given that $A$ is symmetric, so $A^T = A$. We need to show that $(A^2)^T = A^2$.
  
  $(A^2)^T = (A A)^T$
  Using the property $(AB)^T = B^T A^T$, we get:
  $(A A)^T = A^T A^T$
  
  Since $A^T = A$, we can substitute:
  $A^T A^T = A A = A^2$
  
  Therefore, $(A^2)^T = A^2$, which means $A^2$ is symmetric.
### Example 2
  
  Diagonalize the matrix $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 5 \\ 1 & 5 & 1 \\ 5 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ given that $A$ has eigenvalues $-4, 4$, and $7$.
  
  **Solution:**
  
  Since $A$ is symmetric, it is orthogonally diagonalizable. We need to find an orthogonal basis of eigenvectors.
  
  1.  **Find eigenvector for $\lambda_1 = -4$:** Solve $(A - (-4)I)\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$.
    $A + 4I = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 1 & 5 \\ 1 & 9 & 1 \\ 5 & 1 & 5 \end{bmatrix} \sim \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 9 & 1 \\ 5 & 1 & 5 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \sim \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 9 & 1 \\ 0 & -44 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \sim \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$
    $x_1 + x_3 = 0 \implies x_1 = -x_3$. $x_2 = 0$. $x_3$ is free.
    Eigenvector $\mathbf{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$.
  
  2.  **Find eigenvector for $\lambda_2 = 4$:** Solve $(A - 4I)\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$.
    $A - 4I = \begin{bmatrix} -3 & 1 & 5 \\ 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 5 & 1 & -3 \end{bmatrix} \sim \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ -3 & 1 & 5 \\ 5 & 1 & -3 \end{bmatrix} \sim \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 4 & 8 \\ 0 & -4 & -8 \end{bmatrix} \sim \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \sim \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$
    $x_1 - x_3 = 0 \implies x_1 = x_3$. $x_2 + 2x_3 = 0 \implies x_2 = -2x_3$. $x_3$ is free.
    Eigenvector $\mathbf{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$.
  
  3.  **Find eigenvector for $\lambda_3 = 7$:** Solve $(A - 7I)\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$.
    $A - 7I = \begin{bmatrix} -6 & 1 & 5 \\ 1 & -2 & 1 \\ 5 & 1 & -6 \end{bmatrix} \sim \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 & 1 \\ -6 & 1 & 5 \\ 5 & 1 & -6 \end{bmatrix} \sim \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 & 1 \\ 0 & -11 & 11 \\ 0 & 11 & -11 \end{bmatrix} \sim \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \sim \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$
    $x_1 - x_3 = 0 \implies x_1 = x_3$. $x_2 - x_3 = 0 \implies x_2 = x_3$. $x_3$ is free.
    Eigenvector $\mathbf{v}_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$.
  
  4.  **Check Orthogonality (Since A is symmetric, eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues must be orthogonal):**
    $\mathbf{v}_1 \cdot \mathbf{v}_2 = (-1)(1) + (0)(-2) + (1)(1) = -1 + 0 + 1 = 0$
    $\mathbf{v}_1 \cdot \mathbf{v}_3 = (-1)(1) + (0)(1) + (1)(1) = -1 + 0 + 1 = 0$
    $\mathbf{v}_2 \cdot \mathbf{v}_3 = (1)(1) + (-2)(1) + (1)(1) = 1 - 2 + 1 = 0$. They are orthogonal.
  
  5.  **Construct P and D:** For diagonalization $A=PDP^{-1}$, $P$ contains the eigenvectors and $D$ contains the eigenvalues.
    $P = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & -2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$
    $D = \begin{bmatrix} -4 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 4 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 7 \end{bmatrix}$
  
    *(Note: For orthogonal diagonalization, we would normalize the eigenvectors to get an orthogonal matrix P where $P^{-1}=P^T$. But the question just asked to diagonalize.)*
### Theorem
  
  If $A$ is symmetric, then any two eigenvectors from different eigenspaces are orthogonal.
### Example 3
  
  Prove the preceding theorem.
  
  **Solution:**
  
  Let $A$ be a symmetric matrix ($A^T = A$). Let $\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2$ be two distinct eigenvalues, with corresponding eigenvectors $\mathbf{v}_1$ and $\mathbf{v}_2$. So, $A\mathbf{v}_1 = \lambda_1 \mathbf{v}_1$ and $A\mathbf{v}_2 = \lambda_2 \mathbf{v}_2$. We want to show $\mathbf{v}_1 \cdot \mathbf{v}_2 = 0$.
  
  Consider $\lambda_1 (\mathbf{v}_1 \cdot \mathbf{v}_2)$:
  $\lambda_1 (\mathbf{v}_1 \cdot \mathbf{v}_2) = (\lambda_1 \mathbf{v}_1) \cdot \mathbf{v}_2$
  $= (A\mathbf{v}_1) \cdot \mathbf{v}_2$
  $= (A\mathbf{v}_1)^T \mathbf{v}_2$
  $= (\mathbf{v}_1^T A^T) \mathbf{v}_2$
  $= \mathbf{v}_1^T A \mathbf{v}_2$ (since $A^T = A$)
  $= \mathbf{v}_1^T (A \mathbf{v}_2)$
  $= \mathbf{v}_1^T (\lambda_2 \mathbf{v}_2)$
  $= \lambda_2 (\mathbf{v}_1^T \mathbf{v}_2)$
  $= \lambda_2 (\mathbf{v}_1 \cdot \mathbf{v}_2)$
  
  So, we have $\lambda_1 (\mathbf{v}_1 \cdot \mathbf{v}_2) = \lambda_2 (\mathbf{v}_1 \cdot \mathbf{v}_2)$.
  Rearranging gives $(\lambda_1 - \lambda_2) (\mathbf{v}_1 \cdot \mathbf{v}_2) = 0$.
  
  Since the eigenvalues $\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2$ are distinct, $\lambda_1 - \lambda_2 \neq 0$.
  Therefore, we must have $\mathbf{v}_1 \cdot \mathbf{v}_2 = 0$. This shows the eigenvectors are orthogonal.
### Orthogonal Diagonalizability
  
  An $n \times n$ matrix $A$ is **orthogonally diagonalizable** if there exists an orthogonal matrix $P$ and a diagonal matrix $D$ such that $A = PDP^{-1} = PDP^T$.
  
  An $n \times n$ matrix $A$ is orthogonally diagonalizable if and only if $A$ is a symmetric matrix.
### Example 4
  
  Diagonalize the matrix $A = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 8 & -4 \\ 8 & 5 & -4 \\ -4 & -4 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$ given that $A$ has eigenvalues $-3$ and $15$.
  
  **Solution:**
  
  The matrix A is symmetric, so it must be orthogonally diagonalizable.
  
  1.  **Find basis for eigenspace $\lambda_1 = -3$:** Solve $(A - (-3)I)\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$.
    $A + 3I = \begin{bmatrix} 8 & 8 & -4 \\ 8 & 8 & -4 \\ -4 & -4 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \sim \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 2 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$
    $2x_1 + 2x_2 - x_3 = 0 \implies x_3 = 2x_1 + 2x_2$. $x_1, x_2$ are free.
    $\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ 2x_1+2x_2 \end{bmatrix} = x_1 \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} + x_2 \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$.
    Basis for $E_{-3}$: $\left\{ \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} \right\}$. Let these be $\mathbf{w}_1, \mathbf{w}_2$.
  
  2.  **Find basis for eigenspace $\lambda_2 = 15$:** Solve $(A - 15I)\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$.
    $A - 15I = \begin{bmatrix} -10 & 8 & -4 \\ 8 & -10 & -4 \\ -4 & -4 & -16 \end{bmatrix} \sim \begin{bmatrix} 5 & -4 & 2 \\ 4 & -5 & -2 \\ 1 & 1 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \sim \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 4 \\ 5 & -4 & 2 \\ 4 & -5 & -2 \end{bmatrix}$
    $\sim \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 4 \\ 0 & -9 & -18 \\ 0 & -9 & -18 \end{bmatrix} \sim \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 4 \\ 0 & 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \sim \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$
    $x_1 + 2x_3 = 0 \implies x_1 = -2x_3$. $x_2 + 2x_3 = 0 \implies x_2 = -2x_3$. $x_3$ is free.
    Basis for $E_{15}$: $\left\{ \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ -2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \right\}$. Let this be $\mathbf{v}_3$.
  
  3.  **Orthogonalize the basis for $E_{-3}$ using Gram-Schmidt:** The basis we found $\{\mathbf{w}_1, \mathbf{w}_2\}$ is not orthogonal.
    *   Set $\mathbf{v}_1 = \mathbf{w}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$.
    *   $\mathbf{w}_2 \cdot \mathbf{v}_1 = (0)(1) + (1)(0) + (2)(2) = 4$.
    *   $\mathbf{v}_1 \cdot \mathbf{v}_1 = 1^2+0^2+2^2 = 5$.
    *   $\mathbf{v}_2 = \mathbf{w}_2 - \frac{\mathbf{w}_2 \cdot \mathbf{v}_1}{\mathbf{v}_1 \cdot \mathbf{v}_1} \mathbf{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} - \frac{4}{5} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -4/5 \\ 1 \\ 2 - 8/5 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -4/5 \\ 1 \\ 2/5 \end{bmatrix}$.
        Scale $\mathbf{v}_2$ to avoid fractions: $\mathbf{v}_2' = 5\mathbf{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} -4 \\ 5 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$.
    *   Orthogonal basis for $E_{-3}$: $\left\{ \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} -4 \\ 5 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} \right\}$.
  
  4.  **Form the orthogonal basis of eigenvectors:** The full orthogonal basis is $\{\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2', \mathbf{v}_3\} = \left\{ \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} -4 \\ 5 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ -2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \right\}$.
  
  5.  **Construct P and D:** For orthogonal diagonalization, $P$ must have orthonormal columns.
    *   $\|\mathbf{v}_1\| = \sqrt{5}$. $\mathbf{u}_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$.
    *   $\|\mathbf{v}_2'\| = \sqrt{16+25+4} = \sqrt{45} = 3\sqrt{5}$. $\mathbf{u}_2 = \frac{1}{3\sqrt{5}}\begin{bmatrix} -4 \\ 5 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$.
    *   $\|\mathbf{v}_3\| = \sqrt{4+4+1} = \sqrt{9} = 3$. $\mathbf{u}_3 = \frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ -2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$.
    *   $P = \begin{bmatrix} 1/\sqrt{5} & -4/(3\sqrt{5}) & -2/3 \\ 0 & 5/(3\sqrt{5}) & -2/3 \\ 2/\sqrt{5} & 2/(3\sqrt{5}) & 1/3 \end{bmatrix}$
    *   $D = \begin{bmatrix} -3 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & -3 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 15 \end{bmatrix}$
    *   Result: $A = PDP^T$ with $P$ and $D$ above.
### The Spectrum of a Matrix
  
  The set of the eigenvalues of a matrix $A$ is sometimes called the **spectrum** of $A$.
  
  Theorems dealing with the eigenvalues of a matrix are sometimes called **spectral theorems**.
### The Spectral Theorem for Symmetric Matrices
  
  An $n \times n$ symmetric matrix $A$ has the following properties:
  
  a. $A$ has $n$ real eigenvalues, counting multiplicities.
  b. The dimension of the eigenspace for each eigenvalue $\lambda$ equals the multiplicity of $\lambda$ as a root of the characteristic equation.
  c. The eigenspaces of $A$ are mutually orthogonal, in the sense that eigenvectors corresponding to different eigenvalues are orthogonal.
  d. $A$ is orthogonally diagonalizable.
### Spectral Decompositions
  
  Let $A$ be an $n \times n$ symmetric matrix. Then, $A = PDP^{-1} = PDP^T$ where the columns of $P$ are orthonormal eigenvectors $\mathbf{u}_1, ..., \mathbf{u}_n$ corresponding to eigenvalues $\lambda_1, ..., \lambda_n$.
  
  A **spectral decomposition** of $A$ is $A = \lambda_1 \mathbf{u}_1 \mathbf{u}_1^T + ... + \lambda_n \mathbf{u}_n \mathbf{u}_n^T$.
### Example 5
  
  Construct a spectral decomposition of $A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$.
  
  **Solution:**
  
  1.  **Find eigenvalues:**
    $\text{det}(A-\lambda I) = \begin{vmatrix} 3-\lambda & 1 \\ 1 & 3-\lambda \end{vmatrix} = (3-\lambda)^2 - 1 = \lambda^2 - 6\lambda + 9 - 1 = \lambda^2 - 6\lambda + 8 = (\lambda-4)(\lambda-2) = 0$.
    Eigenvalues $\lambda_1 = 4, \lambda_2 = 2$.
  
  2.  **Find eigenvectors:**
    *   $\lambda_1 = 4$: $(A-4I)\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0} \implies x_1 = x_2$. Eigenvector $\mathbf{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$.
    *   $\lambda_2 = 2$: $(A-2I)\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0} \implies x_1 = -x_2$. Eigenvector $\mathbf{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$.
  
  3.  **Normalize eigenvectors:**
    *   $\|\mathbf{v}_1\| = \sqrt{1^2+1^2} = \sqrt{2}$. $\mathbf{u}_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$.
    *   $\|\mathbf{v}_2\| = \sqrt{(-1)^2+1^2} = \sqrt{2}$. $\mathbf{u}_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$.
  
  4.  **Construct spectral decomposition:** $A = \lambda_1 \mathbf{u}_1 \mathbf{u}_1^T + \lambda_2 \mathbf{u}_2 \mathbf{u}_2^T$.
    *   $\mathbf{u}_1 \mathbf{u}_1^T = \left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\right) \left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\right) = \frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$.
    *   $\mathbf{u}_2 \mathbf{u}_2^T = \left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\right) \left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\right) = \frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$.
    *   $A = 4 \left(\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\right) + 2 \left(\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\right) = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$.
  
    Spectral decomposition: $A = 4 \left(\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\right) + 2 \left(\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\right)$.
### Comments on Spectral Decompositions
  
  $A = \lambda_1 \mathbf{u}_1 \mathbf{u}_1^T + ... + \lambda_n \mathbf{u}_n \mathbf{u}_n^T$
  
  Every term in the spectral decomposition is an $n \times n$ matrix of rank 1.
  
  Each matrix $\mathbf{u}_i \mathbf{u}_i^T$ is a projection matrix – in other words, for each $\mathbf{x}$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$, the vector $(\mathbf{u}_i \mathbf{u}_i^T)\mathbf{x}$ is the projection of $\mathbf{x}$ onto $\mathbf{u}_i$.
