## Section 6.1: Inner Product, Length, and Orthogonality
### The (Usual) Inner Product
  
  If $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ are vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$, then the (usual) **inner product** of $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ is:
  
  $\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{u}^T \mathbf{v}$
  
  which is a $1 \times 1$ matrix, generally regarded as a scalar.
  
  The (usual) inner product of $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ is often referred to as the **dot product** of $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$.
### Example 1
  
  Compute $\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}$ and $\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{u}$ for $\mathbf{u} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -3 \\ 7 \end{bmatrix}$ and $\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 5 \\ -4 \end{bmatrix}$.
  
  **Solution:**
  
  $\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{u}^T \mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -3 & 7 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 5 \\ -4 \end{bmatrix} = (1)(2) + (-3)(5) + (7)(-4) = 2 - 15 - 28 = -41$.
  
  $\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{u} = \mathbf{v}^T \mathbf{u} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 5 & -4 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -3 \\ 7 \end{bmatrix} = (2)(1) + (5)(-3) + (-4)(7) = 2 - 15 - 28 = -41$.
  
  As expected, $\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{u}$.
### Properties of the Inner Product
  
  Let $\mathbf{u}$, $\mathbf{v}$, and $\mathbf{w}$ be vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$, and let $c$ be a scalar. Then,
  
  a. $\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{u}$
  b. $(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) \cdot \mathbf{w} = \mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{w} + \mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{w}$
  c. $(c\mathbf{u}) \cdot \mathbf{v} = c(\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}) = \mathbf{u} \cdot (c\mathbf{v})$
  d. $\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{u} \geq 0$, and $\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{u} = 0$ if and only if $\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{0}$
### The Norm of a Vector
  
  The **norm** of a vector $\mathbf{v}$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$ is the nonnegative scalar:
  
  $\lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert = \sqrt{\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{v}}$
  
  This gives us the magnitude (length) of $\mathbf{v}$.
### Unit Vectors
  
  A vector with norm (length) 1 is a **unit vector**.
  
  Dividing a vector $\mathbf{v}$ by its norm creates a unit vector in the same direction as $\mathbf{v}$; this is called **normalizing** the vector.
### Example 2
  
  Calculate $\lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert$ given $\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 6 \\ -2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}$. Then, find a unit vector $\mathbf{u}$ in the direction of $\mathbf{v}$.
  
  **Solution:**
  
  1.  **Calculate the norm:**
    $\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{v} = (6)(6) + (-2)(-2) + (3)(3) = 36 + 4 + 9 = 49$.
    $\lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert = \sqrt{\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{v}} = \sqrt{49} = 7$.
  
  2.  **Normalize the vector:** Divide $\mathbf{v}$ by its norm.
    $\mathbf{u} = \frac{1}{\lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert} \mathbf{v} = \frac{1}{7} \begin{bmatrix} 6 \\ -2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 6/7 \\ -2/7 \\ 3/7 \end{bmatrix}$.
### Distances in $\mathbb{R}^n$
  
  If $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ are vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$, the distance between $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ is the length of the vector $\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{v}$. Thus,
  
  $\text{dist}(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}) = \lVert \mathbf{u} - \mathbf{v} \rVert$
### Example 3
  
  Two lines through the origin in $\mathbb{R}^2$ (or $\mathbb{R}^3$) determined by vectors $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ are perpendicular if and only if the distance from $\mathbf{u}$ to $\mathbf{v}$ is the same as the distance from $\mathbf{u}$ to $-\mathbf{v}$, as shown in the figure.
  
  ![Example 3 Diagram](https://i.imgur.com/j49hZl8.png)
  
  Use this to show that $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ are perpendicular if and only if $\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = 0$.
  
  **Solution:**
  
  The distance from $\mathbf{u}$ to $\mathbf{v}$ is $\text{dist}(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}) = \lVert \mathbf{u} - \mathbf{v} \rVert$.
  The distance from $\mathbf{u}$ to $-\mathbf{v}$ is $\text{dist}(\mathbf{u}, -\mathbf{v}) = \lVert \mathbf{u} - (-\mathbf{v}) \rVert = \lVert \mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} \rVert$.
  
  The condition that the distances are equal is:
  $\lVert \mathbf{u} - \mathbf{v} \rVert = \lVert \mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} \rVert$
  
  Squaring both sides (since distance is nonnegative):
  $\lVert \mathbf{u} - \mathbf{v} \rVert^2 = \lVert \mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} \rVert^2$
  
  Using the property $\lVert \mathbf{w} \rVert^2 = \mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{w}$:
  $(\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{v}) \cdot (\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{v}) = (\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) \cdot (\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v})$
  
  Expand using the properties of the dot product:
  $\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{u} - \mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} - \mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{u} + \mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} + \mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{v}$
  $\lVert \mathbf{u} \rVert^2 - 2(\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}) + \lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert^2 = \lVert \mathbf{u} \rVert^2 + 2(\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}) + \lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert^2$
  
  Subtract $\lVert \mathbf{u} \rVert^2$ and $\lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert^2$ from both sides:
  $- 2(\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}) = 2(\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v})$
  $0 = 4(\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v})$
  $0 = \mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}$
  
  Thus, the geometric condition of equal distances is equivalent to the algebraic condition $\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = 0$.
### Orthogonality
  
  Two vectors $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$ are **orthogonal** if and only if $\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = 0$.
  
  Equivalently, two vectors $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$ are orthogonal if and only if $\lVert \mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} \rVert^2 = \lVert \mathbf{u} \rVert^2 + \lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert^2$ (The Pythagorean Theorem).
### Example 4
  
  Calculate the distance between $\mathbf{u} = \begin{bmatrix} 12 \\ 3 \\ -5 \end{bmatrix}$ and $\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -3 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}$.
  
  Are $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ orthogonal?
  
  **Solution:**
  
  1.  **Calculate $\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{v}$:**
    $\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 12 - 2 \\ 3 - (-3) \\ -5 - 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 10 \\ 6 \\ -8 \end{bmatrix}$
  
  2.  **Calculate the distance:**
    $\text{dist}(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}) = \lVert \mathbf{u} - \mathbf{v} \rVert = \sqrt{(10)^2 + (6)^2 + (-8)^2} = \sqrt{100 + 36 + 64} = \sqrt{200} = \sqrt{100 \times 2} = 10\sqrt{2}$.
  
  3.  **Check for orthogonality:**
    $\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = (12)(2) + (3)(-3) + (-5)(3) = 24 - 9 - 15 = 0$.
    Since $\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = 0$, the vectors $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ **are** orthogonal.
### Example 5
  
  True or False. The zero vector in $\mathbb{R}^n$ is orthogonal to every vector in $\mathbb{R}^n$.
  
  **Solution:**
  
  **True.** Let $\mathbf{v}$ be any vector in $\mathbb{R}^n$. Then $\mathbf{0} \cdot \mathbf{v} = 0 \cdot v_1 + 0 \cdot v_2 + ... + 0 \cdot v_n = 0$. Since the dot product is zero, the zero vector is orthogonal to $\mathbf{v}$.
### Orthogonal Complements
  
  If a vector $\mathbf{v}$ is orthogonal to every vector in a subspace $W$ of $\mathbb{R}^n$, then $\mathbf{v}$ is orthogonal to $W$.
  
  The set of all vectors $\mathbf{v}$ which are orthogonal to $W$ forms the **orthogonal complement** of $W$, denoted $W^\perp$ (and read as "$W$ perp").
### Orthogonal Complements (Continued)
  
  A vector $\mathbf{v}$ is in $W^\perp$ if and only if $\mathbf{v}$ is orthogonal to every vector in a basis of $W$.
  
  Note that $W^\perp$ is a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^n$.
### Example 6
  
  Let $W$ be a plane through the origin in $\mathbb{R}^3$, and let $L$ be the line through the origin perpendicular to $W$. Describe $W^\perp$ and $L^\perp$.
  
  **Solution:**
  
  *   **Describe $W^\perp$:** $W^\perp$ is the set of all vectors orthogonal to the plane $W$. Since $L$ is the line perpendicular (orthogonal) to the plane $W$, all vectors on the line $L$ are orthogonal to every vector in $W$. Therefore, $W^\perp = L$.
  
  *   **Describe $L^\perp$:** $L^\perp$ is the set of all vectors orthogonal to the line $L$. Since every vector in the plane $W$ is perpendicular (orthogonal) to the line $L$, $L^\perp = W$.
### Theorem
  
  Let $A$ be an $m \times n$ matrix. The orthogonal complement of the row space of $A$ is the null space of $A$, and the orthogonal complement of the column space of $A$ is the null space of $A^T$.
  
  $(\text{Row } A)^\perp = \text{Nul } A$
  $(\text{Col } A)^\perp = \text{Nul } A^T$
