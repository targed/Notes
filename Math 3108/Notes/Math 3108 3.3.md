## Section 3.3: Cramer's Rule and Volume
### Cramer's Rule
  
  Let $A$ be an invertible $n \times n$ matrix. For any $\mathbf{b}$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$, the unique solution $\mathbf{x}$ of $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ has entries given by:
  
  $x_i = \frac{\text{det } A_i(\mathbf{b})}{\text{det } A}$
  
  where $A_i(\mathbf{b})$ is the matrix obtained by replacing column $i$ of $A$ with $\mathbf{b}$.
### Example 1
  
  Prove Cramer's Rule.
  
  **Solution:**
  
  Let $A$ be an invertible $n\times n$ matrix and let $\mathbf{b}$ be in $\mathbb{R}^n$.  We want to show that the unique solution $\mathbf{x}$ to $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ is given by the formula in Cramer's rule.
  We have
  $A =  \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{a_1} &  \mathbf{a_2} & ... &  \mathbf{a_n}  \end{bmatrix}$.
  Let $I$ denote an identity matrix of size n x n.
  Consider, $AI_i(\mathbf{x})$.
  $AI_i(\mathbf{x}) =  \begin{bmatrix} A\mathbf{e_1} &  A\mathbf{e_2} & ... &  A\mathbf{x} &...& A\mathbf{e_n}  \end{bmatrix}$
  $\begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{a_1} &  \mathbf{a_2} & ... &  \mathbf{b} &...&  \mathbf{a_n}  \end{bmatrix} = A_i(\mathbf{b})$.
  Taking the determinant:
  $det(AI_i(\mathbf{x}))= det(A_i(\mathbf{b}))$
  We also know $det(AB) = det(A)det(B)$
  $det(A)det(I_i(\mathbf{x}))= det(A_i(\mathbf{b}))$
  Notice that $I_i(x)$ has the same structure of the identity matrix, but with a column vector x in a column, and zeros everywhere else in that column. So, using a cofactor expansion across that column, we get:
  $det(I_i(\mathbf{x})) = x_i$
  $det(A)x_i = det(A_i(\mathbf{b}))$
  $x_i = \frac{det(A_i(\mathbf{b}))}{det(A)}$
### Example 2
  
  Use Cramer's Rule to solve the system:
  
  $2x_1 + 6x_2 = -1$
  $x_1 + 8x_2 = 2$
  
  **Solution:**
  
  1.  **Write the system in matrix form:**
    $\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 6 \\ 1 & 8 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$
  
  2.  **Calculate det A:**
    $\text{det } A = \begin{vmatrix} 2 & 6 \\ 1 & 8 \end{vmatrix} = (2)(8) - (6)(1) = 16 - 6 = 10$
  
  3.  **Calculate det $A_1(\mathbf{b})$:**
    $A_1(\mathbf{b}) = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 6 \\ 2 & 8 \end{bmatrix}$
    $\text{det } A_1(\mathbf{b}) = (-1)(8) - (6)(2) = -8 - 12 = -20$
  
  4.  **Calculate det $A_2(\mathbf{b})$:**
    $A_2(\mathbf{b}) = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$
    $\text{det } A_2(\mathbf{b}) = (2)(2) - (-1)(1) = 4 + 1 = 5$
  
  5.  **Apply Cramer's Rule:**
    $x_1 = \frac{\text{det } A_1(\mathbf{b})}{\text{det } A} = \frac{-20}{10} = -2$
    $x_2 = \frac{\text{det } A_2(\mathbf{b})}{\text{det } A} = \frac{5}{10} = \frac{1}{2}$
  
    Solution: $x_1 = -2$, $x_2 = \frac{1}{2}$
### Example 3
  
  Determine the values of the parameter $s$ for which the system has a unique solution, and describe the solution.
  
  $3sx_1 + 5x_2 = 3$
  $12x_1 + 5sx_2 = 2$
  
  **Solution:**
  We can represent the system as a matrix:
  $A = \begin{bmatrix} 3s & 5 \\ 12 & 5s \end{bmatrix}$
  $b =  \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$
  
  $det(A) = 15s^2 - 60$
  For there to be a unique solution, the matrix needs to be invertible. This means the determinant must not be zero.
  $15s^2 - 60 \neq 0$
  $s^2 \neq 4$
  $s\neq \pm 2$
  
  Now we can solve using cramer's rule.
  $x_1 = \frac{\begin{vmatrix} 3 & 5 \\ 2 & 5s \end{vmatrix}}{15s^2 - 60} = \frac{15s-10}{15s^2 - 60}$
  $x_2 = \frac{\begin{vmatrix} 3s & 3 \\ 12 & 2 \end{vmatrix}}{15s^2 - 60} = \frac{6s-36}{15s^2 - 60}$
### Cramer's Rule and Inverses
  
  To find $A^{-1}$, we typically row reduce the matrix $\begin{bmatrix} A & I \end{bmatrix}$ to get $\begin{bmatrix} I & A^{-1} \end{bmatrix}$.
  
  Thus, the $j$th column of $A^{-1}$ is a vector $\mathbf{x}$ satisfying $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{e}_j$, where $\mathbf{e}_j$ is the $j$th column of $I$.
  
  The $i$th entry of $\mathbf{x}$ is the $(i, j)$ entry of $A^{-1}$.
  
  By Cramer's rule:
  
  $x_i = \frac{\text{det } A_i(\mathbf{e}_j)}{\text{det } A}$
  
  If we evaluate $\text{det } A_i(\mathbf{e}_j)$ by a cofactor expansion down column $i$, we see that:
  
  $\text{det } A_i(\mathbf{e}_j) = (-1)^{i+j}\text{det } A_{ji} = C_{ji}$
  
  Thus, the $(i, j)$ entry of $A^{-1}$ is $\frac{C_{ji}}{\text{det } A}$, and we can express $A^{-1}$ as:
  
  $A^{-1} = \frac{1}{\text{det } A} \begin{bmatrix} C_{11} & C_{21} & ... & C_{n1} \\ C_{12} & C_{22} & ... & C_{n2} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ C_{1n} & C_{2n} & ... & C_{nn} \end{bmatrix}$
  
  The matrix of cofactors is called the **adjugate** (or **classical adjoint**) of $A$.
  
  Thus, $A^{-1} = \frac{1}{\text{det } A} \text{adj } A$.
### Example 4
  
  Compute the adjugate of the matrix and use it to find the inverse.
  
  $A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 5 & 4 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 2 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$
  
  **Solution:**
  
  1.  **Compute the matrix of cofactors:**
  
    $C_{11} = (-1)^{1+1}\begin{vmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{vmatrix} = (1)(0-1) = -1$
    $C_{12} = (-1)^{1+2}\begin{vmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 2 & 1 \end{vmatrix} = (-1)(1-2) = 1$
    $C_{13} = (-1)^{1+3}\begin{vmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 \end{vmatrix} = (1)(1-0) = 1$
    $C_{21} = (-1)^{2+1}\begin{vmatrix} 5 & 4 \\ 1 & 1 \end{vmatrix} = (-1)(5-4) = -1$
    $C_{22} = (-1)^{2+2}\begin{vmatrix} 3 & 4 \\ 2 & 1 \end{vmatrix} = (1)(3-8) = -5$
    $C_{23} = (-1)^{2+3}\begin{vmatrix} 3 & 5 \\ 2 & 1 \end{vmatrix} = (-1)(3-10) = 7$
    $C_{31} = (-1)^{3+1}\begin{vmatrix} 5 & 4 \\ 0 & 1 \end{vmatrix} = (1)(5-0) = 5$
    $C_{32} = (-1)^{3+2}\begin{vmatrix} 3 & 4 \\ 1 & 1 \end{vmatrix} = (-1)(3-4) = 1$
    $C_{33} = (-1)^{3+3}\begin{vmatrix} 3 & 5 \\ 1 & 0 \end{vmatrix} = (1)(0-5) = -5$
  
    Matrix of cofactors: $\begin{bmatrix} -1 & 1 & 1 \\ -1 & -5 & 7 \\ 5 & 1 & -5 \end{bmatrix}$
  
  2.  **Compute the adjugate (transpose of the cofactor matrix):**
  
    $\text{adj } A = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & -1 & 5 \\ 1 & -5 & 1 \\ 1 & 7 & -5 \end{bmatrix}$
  
  3.  **Compute det A:**
  
    $\text{det } A = (3)\begin{vmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{vmatrix} - (5)\begin{vmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 2 & 1\end{vmatrix} + (4)\begin{vmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 \end{vmatrix} = (3)(-1) - (5)(-1) + (4)(1) = -3 + 5 + 4 = 6$
  
  4. **Find the inverse using $A^{-1}=\frac{1}{det(A)}adj(A)$:**
  $A^{-1} = \frac{1}{6} \begin{bmatrix} -1 & -1 & 5 \\ 1 & -5 & 1 \\ 1 & 7 & -5 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -1/6 & -1/6 & 5/6 \\ 1/6 & -5/6 & 1/6 \\ 1/6 & 7/6 & -5/6 \end{bmatrix}$
### Warning
  
  Questions about the adjugate (classical adjoint) have been known to pop up on the FE exam!
  
  If you remember that $A^{-1} = \frac{1}{\text{det } A} \text{adj } A$, then you can calculate the adjugate as $\text{adj } A = (\text{det } A)A^{-1}$, where $A^{-1}$ was calculated by any method.
### Determinants and Volume
  
  *   If $A$ is a $2 \times 2$ matrix, the area of the parallelogram determined by the columns of $A$ is $|\text{det } A|$.
  *   If $A$ is a $3 \times 3$ matrix, the volume of the parallelepiped determined by the columns of $A$ is $|\text{det } A|$.
### Example 5
  
  Find the volume of the parallelepiped with one vertex at the origin and adjacent vertices at $(1, 3, 0)$, $(-2, 0, 2)$, and $(-1, 3, -1)$.
  
  **Solution:**
  
  1.  **Form the matrix A using the adjacent vertices as columns:**
  
    $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 & -1 \\ 3 & 0 & 3 \\ 0 & 2 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$
  
  2.  **Calculate the determinant of A:**
  
    $\text{det } A = (1)\begin{vmatrix} 0 & 3 \\ 2 & -1 \end{vmatrix} - (-2)\begin{vmatrix} 3 & 3 \\ 0 & -1 \end{vmatrix} + (-1)\begin{vmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{vmatrix}$
  $ = (1)(0-6) + (2)(-3-0) + (-1)(6-0) = -6 -6 -6 = -18$
  
  3.  **Take the absolute value:**
  
    Volume $= |\text{det } A| = |-18| = 18$
