## Section 3.2: Properties of Determinants
### Row Operations and Determinants
  
  Let $A$ be a square matrix.
  
  *   If a multiple of one row of $A$ is added to another row to produce a matrix $B$, then $\text{det } B = \text{det } A$.
  *   If two rows of $A$ are interchanged to produce $B$, then $\text{det } B = -\text{det } A$.
  *   If one row of $A$ is multiplied by $k$ to produce $B$, then $\text{det } B = k \cdot \text{det } A$.
### Example 1
  
  Find the determinants:
  
  $\begin{vmatrix} -2 & -2 & 2 \\ -6 & -5 & 5 \\ -6 & 9 & 15 \end{vmatrix}$,
  $\begin{vmatrix} 3 & 3 & -3 \\ 3 & 4 & -4 \\ 2 & -3 & -5 \end{vmatrix}$
  
  **Solution:**
  
  **First Matrix:**
  
  $\begin{vmatrix} -2 & -2 & 2 \\ -6 & -5 & 5 \\ -6 & 9 & 15 \end{vmatrix}$
  
  Factor out a -2 from the first row:
  $-2\begin{vmatrix} 1 & 1 & -1 \\ -6 & -5 & 5 \\ -6 & 9 & 15 \end{vmatrix}$
  
  $R_2 \rightarrow R_2 + 6R_1$:
  $-2\begin{vmatrix} 1 & 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 & -1 \\ -6 & 9 & 15 \end{vmatrix}$
  
  $R_3 \rightarrow R_3 + 6R_1$:
  $-2\begin{vmatrix} 1 & 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 15 & 9 \end{vmatrix}$
  
  $R_3 \rightarrow R_3 - 15R_2$:
  $-2\begin{vmatrix} 1 & 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & 24 \end{vmatrix}$
  
  This is now an upper triangular matrix.  The determinant is the product of the diagonal entries:
  $(-2)(1)(1)(24) = -48$
  
  **Second Matrix:**
  
  $\begin{vmatrix} 3 & 3 & -3 \\ 3 & 4 & -4 \\ 2 & -3 & -5 \end{vmatrix}$
  
  Factor a 3 out of the first row:
  
  $= 3\begin{vmatrix} 1 & 1 & -1 \\ 3 & 4 & -4 \\ 2 & -3 & -5 \end{vmatrix}$
  
  $R_2 \rightarrow R_2 - 3R_1$:
  
  $= 3\begin{vmatrix} 1 & 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 & -1 \\ 2 & -3 & -5 \end{vmatrix}$
  
  $R_3 \rightarrow R_3 - 2R_1$:
  
  $= 3\begin{vmatrix} 1 & 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 & -1 \\ 0 & -5 & -3 \end{vmatrix}$
  $R_3 \rightarrow R_3 + 5R_2$
  
  $= 3\begin{vmatrix} 1 & 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & -8 \end{vmatrix}$
  
  This is now an upper triangular matrix. The determinant is the product of the diagonal entries:
  $ = (3)(1)(1)(-8) = -24$
### Example 2
  
  Find the determinant:
  
  $\begin{vmatrix} 2 & 5 & 4 & 1 \\ 4 & 7 & 6 & 2 \\ 6 & -2 & -4 & 0 \\ -6 & 7 & 7 & 0 \end{vmatrix}$
  
  **Solution:**
  
  $R_2 \rightarrow R_2 - 2R_1$:
  
  $= \begin{vmatrix} 2 & 5 & 4 & 1 \\ 0 & -3 & -2 & 0 \\ 6 & -2 & -4 & 0 \\ -6 & 7 & 7 & 0 \end{vmatrix}$
  
  Expand using cofactors along the fourth column.
  $= (-1)^{1+4}(1)\begin{vmatrix} 0 & -3 & -2 \\ 6 & -2 & -4 \\ -6 & 7 & 7 \end{vmatrix}$
  
  $R_3 \rightarrow R_3 + R_2$
  
  $= (-1)\begin{vmatrix} 0 & -3 & -2 \\ 6 & -2 & -4 \\ 0 & 5 & 3 \end{vmatrix}$
  
  Expand across the first column.
  $= (-1)(-1)^{2+1}(6)\begin{vmatrix} -3 & -2 \\ 5 & 3 \end{vmatrix}$
  
  $ = (-1)(-1)(6)((-3)(3) - (-2)(5))$
  $ = (6)(-9+10) = 6$
### Row Operations and Determinants
  
  Suppose a square matrix $A$ has been row reduced to an echelon form $U$ by replacements and interchanges only.
  
  If there are $r$ interchanges, then:
  
  $\text{det } A = (-1)^r \text{det } U$
  
  Since $U$ is in echelon form, it is upper triangular, and thus $\text{det } U$ is the product of the diagonal entries.
  
  If $A$ is singular, at least one row of $U$ will be all zeros, so there will be at least one zero on the diagonal and thus $\text{det } U$ (and $\text{det } A$) will be zero.
### Numerical Note
  
  Evaluation of an $n \times n$ determinant using row operations requires about $2n^3/3$ arithmetic operations.
  
  A modern computer can calculate a $25 \times 25$ determinant in a fraction of a second, since only about 10,000 operations are required.
### Theorems
  
  Let $A$ and $B$ be $n \times n$ matrices. Then,
  
  *   $A$ is invertible if and only if $\text{det } A \neq 0$.
  *   $\text{det } A^T = \text{det } A$.
  *   $\text{det } AB = (\text{det } A)(\text{det } B)$.
### Example 3
  
  Let $A$ and $B$ be square matrices. Show that even though $AB$ and $BA$ may not be equal, it is always true that $\text{det } AB = \text{det } BA$.
  
  **Solution:**
  
  $\text{det } AB = (\text{det } A)(\text{det } B)$
  
  $\text{det } BA = (\text{det } B)(\text{det } A)$
  
  Since scalar multiplication is commutative, $(\text{det } A)(\text{det } B) = (\text{det } B)(\text{det } A)$.  Therefore, $\text{det } AB = \text{det } BA$.
### Example 4
  
  True or False. If the columns of a square matrix $A$ are linearly dependent, then $\text{det } A = 0$.
  
  **Solution:**
  
  **True.** If the columns of $A$ are linearly dependent, then $A$ is *not* invertible.  If $A$ is not invertible, then $\text{det } A = 0$.
