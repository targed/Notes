## Section 2.4: Partitioned Matrices
### Partitioned Matrices
  
  A **partitioned matrix** (or **block matrix**) is a matrix that is interpreted as having been divided into sections called **blocks** (or **submatrices**).
  
  **Example:**
  
  When we view a matrix as a collection of column vectors, we have partitioned the matrix into blocks (the vectors).
### An Example of a Partitioned Matrix
  
  The matrix
  
  $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 4 & 2 & -1 & 3 \\ 5 & -2 & 3 & 1 & 1 \\ -6 & 4 & 1 & 2 & 9 \\ 7 & 1 & 3 & 1 & -5 \end{bmatrix}$
  
  can be partitioned as
  
  $A = \begin{bmatrix} \begin{array}{ccc|c|c} 1 & 4 & 2 & -1 & 3 \\ 5 & -2 & 3 & 1 & 1 \\ \hline -6 & 4 & 1 & 2 & 9 \\ \hline 7 & 1 & 3 & 1 & -5 \end{array} \end{bmatrix}$
### Addition of Partitioned Matrices
  
  If matrices $A$ and $B$ are the same size and are partitioned in exactly the same way, then the sum $A + B$ (which is also the same size) can also be partitioned the same way, and each block of $A + B$ can be computed separately as the sum of the corresponding blocks of $A$ and $B$.
### Multiplication of Partitioned Matrices
  
  Partitioned matrices can be multiplied by the usual row-column rule as if the blocks were scalars, provided that the column partition of the first matrix matches the row partition of the second. Such partitions are said to be **conformable for block multiplication**.
### Example 1
  
  Assume the matrices are partitioned conformably for block multiplication. Compute the product:
  
  $\begin{bmatrix} I & 0 \\ -X & I \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} A & B \\ C & D \end{bmatrix}$
  
  **Solution:**
  
  We treat the blocks as if they were scalars and perform matrix multiplication:
  
  $\begin{bmatrix} I & 0 \\ -X & I \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} A & B \\ C & D \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} (I)(A) + (0)(C) & (I)(B) + (0)(D) \\ (-X)(A) + (I)(C) & (-X)(B) + (I)(D) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} A & B \\ -XA + C & -XB + D \end{bmatrix}$
### Example 2
  
  Find formulas for $X$, $Y$, and $Z$ in terms of $A$, $B$, and $C$. Justify your calculations. In some cases, you may have to make assumptions about the size of a matrix to produce a formula.
  
  $\begin{bmatrix} A & B \\ C & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} I & 0 \\ X & Y \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & I \\ Z & 0 \end{bmatrix}$
  
  **Solution:**
  
  Performing block multiplication on the left side:
  
  $\begin{bmatrix} A & B \\ C & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} I & 0 \\ X & Y \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} AI + BX & AY + BY \\ CI + 0X & C0 + 0Y \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} A + BX & AY + BY \\ C & 0 \end{bmatrix}$
  
  Now, we equate the corresponding blocks with the right-hand side matrix:
  
  $\begin{bmatrix} A + BX & AY+BY \\ C & 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & I \\ Z & 0 \end{bmatrix}$
  
  This gives us the following equations:
  
  1.  $A + BX = 0$
  2.  $AY + BY = I -> (A+B)Y = I$
  3.  $C = Z$
  
  From equation (1), $BX = -A$.  If $B$ is invertible, then $X = -B^{-1}A$.
  From equation (2), if A+B is invertible, Y = (A+B)^-1
  From equation (3), $Z = C$.
  
  Therefore, assuming $B$ and A+B are invertible:  $X = -B^{-1}A$, $Y = (A+B)^{-1}$, and $Z = C$.
### The Column-Row Expansion of AB
  
  Let $A$ be an $m \times n$ matrix and $B$ be an $n \times p$ matrix. Then,
  
  $AB = \begin{bmatrix} \text{col}_1(A) & \text{col}_2(A) & ... & \text{col}_n(A) \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \text{row}_1(B) \\ \text{row}_2(B) \\ \vdots \\ \text{row}_n(B) \end{bmatrix} = \text{col}_1(A)\text{row}_1(B) + ... + \text{col}_n(A)\text{row}_n(B)$
  
  Each term in this expansion is called an **outer product**.
### Example 3
  
  Let $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \end{bmatrix}$ and $B = \begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \\ e & f \end{bmatrix}$.
  
  Verify that $AB$ can be calculated as:
  
  $\text{col}_1(A)\text{row}_1(B) + \text{col}_2(A)\text{row}_2(B) + \text{col}_3(A)\text{row}_3(B)$
  
  **Solution:**
  
  First, calculate $AB$ directly:
  
  $AB = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \\ e & f \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} a + 2c + 3e & b + 2d + 3f \\ 4a + 5c + 6e & 4b + 5d + 6f \end{bmatrix}$
  
  Now, calculate the column-row expansion:
  
  $\text{col}_1(A)\text{row}_1(B) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} a & b \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ 4a & 4b \end{bmatrix}$
  
  $\text{col}_2(A)\text{row}_2(B) = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} c & d \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2c & 2d \\ 5c & 5d \end{bmatrix}$
  
  $\text{col}_3(A)\text{row}_3(B) = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} e & f \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3e & 3f \\ 6e & 6f \end{bmatrix}$
  
  Adding these together:
  
  $\begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ 4a & 4b \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 2c & 2d \\ 5c & 5d \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 3e & 3f \\ 6e & 6f \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} a + 2c + 3e & b + 2d + 3f \\ 4a + 5c + 6e & 4b + 5d + 6f \end{bmatrix}$
  
  This matches the direct calculation of $AB$.
### Inverses of Partitioned Matrices
  
  A matrix of the form $A = \begin{bmatrix} A_{11} & A_{12} \\ 0 & A_{22} \end{bmatrix}$ is called **block upper triangular**.
  
  The inverse of such a matrix is:
  
  $A^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} A_{11}^{-1} & -A_{11}^{-1}A_{12}A_{22}^{-1} \\ 0 & A_{22}^{-1} \end{bmatrix}$
  
  A **block diagonal matrix** is a partitioned matrix where all blocks which are not along the main diagonal of blocks are zero.
  
  The inverse of a block diagonal matrix is:
  $A = \begin{bmatrix} A_{11} & 0 \\ 0 & A_{22} \end{bmatrix}$, then $A^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} A_{11}^{-1} & 0 \\ 0 & A_{22}^{-1} \end{bmatrix}$
### Example 4
  
  Without using row reduction, find the inverse of the matrix:
  
  $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 \\ 3 & 4 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 5 \end{bmatrix}$
  
  **Solution:**
  
  This is a block diagonal matrix: $A = \begin{bmatrix} A_{11} & 0 \\ 0 & A_{22} \end{bmatrix}$, where $A_{11} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$ and $A_{22} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \end{bmatrix}$.
  
  1.  **Find the inverse of $A_{11}$:**
    $\text{det } A_{11} = (1)(4) - (2)(3) = -2$
    $A_{11}^{-1} = \frac{1}{-2} \begin{bmatrix} 4 & -2 \\ -3 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -2 & 1 \\ \frac{3}{2} & -\frac{1}{2} \end{bmatrix}$
  
  2.  **Find the inverse of $A_{22}$:**
    $A_{22}^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{1}{5} \end{bmatrix}$
  
  3.  **Construct the inverse of A:**
  
    $A^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} A_{11}^{-1} & 0 \\ 0 & A_{22}^{-1} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -2 & 1 & 0 \\ \frac{3}{2} & -\frac{1}{2} & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & \frac{1}{5} \end{bmatrix}$
### Numerical Notes
  
  *   When matrices are too large to fit in a computer's high-speed memory, partitioning permits the computer to work with only a small number of submatrices (typically 2-3) at a time.
  *   Professional software for high-performance numerical linear algebra makes intensive use of partitioned matrix calculations.
  *   A research team was working on a problem which initially had 837 equations and over 12,750,000 variables. They were able to partition the resulting matrix into 837 rows and 51 block columns, each of which contained about 250,000 individual columns. This allowed them to solve the problem in about 4 minutes on a supercomputer.
