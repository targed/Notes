## Section 6.2: Orthogonal Sets
### Orthogonal Sets
  
  A set of vectors $\{\mathbf{u}_1, ..., \mathbf{u}_p\}$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$ is said to be an **orthogonal set** if each pair of distinct vectors in the set is orthogonal; that is, if $\mathbf{u}_i \cdot \mathbf{u}_j = 0$ whenever $i \neq j$.
### Theorem
  
  If $S = \{\mathbf{u}_1, ..., \mathbf{u}_p\}$ is an orthogonal set of nonzero vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$, then $S$ is linearly independent and hence is a basis for the subspace spanned by $S$.
### Orthogonal Bases
  
  An **orthogonal basis** for a subspace $W$ of $\mathbb{R}^n$ is a basis for $W$ that is also an orthogonal set.
### Theorem
  
  Let $\{\mathbf{u}_1, ..., \mathbf{u}_p\}$ be an orthogonal basis for a subspace $W$ of $\mathbb{R}^n$. Then, each $\mathbf{y}$ in $W$ can be written as:
  
  $\mathbf{y} = c_1\mathbf{u}_1 + ... + c_p\mathbf{u}_p$
  
  where the weights are given by:
  
  $c_j = \frac{\mathbf{y} \cdot \mathbf{u}_j}{\mathbf{u}_j \cdot \mathbf{u}_j}$
### Example 1
  
  Show that $\{\mathbf{u}_1, \mathbf{u}_2, \mathbf{u}_3\}$ is an orthogonal basis for $\mathbb{R}^3$. Then express $\mathbf{x}$ as a linear combination of $\mathbf{u}_1, \mathbf{u}_2, \mathbf{u}_3$.
  
  $\mathbf{u}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \mathbf{u}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 4 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \mathbf{u}_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix}, \mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 8 \\ -4 \\ -3 \end{bmatrix}$
  
  **Solution:**
  
  1.  **Check Orthogonality:**
    $\mathbf{u}_1 \cdot \mathbf{u}_2 = (1)(-1) + (0)(4) + (1)(1) = -1 + 0 + 1 = 0$
    $\mathbf{u}_1 \cdot \mathbf{u}_3 = (1)(2) + (0)(1) + (1)(-2) = 2 + 0 - 2 = 0$
    $\mathbf{u}_2 \cdot \mathbf{u}_3 = (-1)(2) + (4)(1) + (1)(-2) = -2 + 4 - 2 = 0$
    Since all pairs of distinct vectors are orthogonal, the set is orthogonal.
  
  2.  **Check Basis for $\mathbb{R}^3$:** Since we have 3 orthogonal vectors in $\mathbb{R}^3$, and orthogonal sets of nonzero vectors are linearly independent, these 3 vectors form a linearly independent set in $\mathbb{R}^3$. A set of 3 linearly independent vectors in $\mathbb{R}^3$ automatically forms a basis for $\mathbb{R}^3$. Thus, it is an orthogonal basis for $\mathbb{R}^3$.
  
  3.  **Calculate Weights:**
    $c_1 = \frac{\mathbf{x} \cdot \mathbf{u}_1}{\mathbf{u}_1 \cdot \mathbf{u}_1} = \frac{(8)(1) + (-4)(0) + (-3)(1)}{(1)(1) + (0)(0) + (1)(1)} = \frac{8 - 3}{1 + 1} = \frac{5}{2}$
    $c_2 = \frac{\mathbf{x} \cdot \mathbf{u}_2}{\mathbf{u}_2 \cdot \mathbf{u}_2} = \frac{(8)(-1) + (-4)(4) + (-3)(1)}{(-1)(-1) + (4)(4) + (1)(1)} = \frac{-8 - 16 - 3}{1 + 16 + 1} = \frac{-27}{18} = -\frac{3}{2}$
    $c_3 = \frac{\mathbf{x} \cdot \mathbf{u}_3}{\mathbf{u}_3 \cdot \mathbf{u}_3} = \frac{(8)(2) + (-4)(1) + (-3)(-2)}{(2)(2) + (1)(1) + (-2)(-2)} = \frac{16 - 4 + 6}{4 + 1 + 4} = \frac{18}{9} = 2$
  
  4.  **Express $\mathbf{x}$ as a linear combination:**
    $\mathbf{x} = c_1\mathbf{u}_1 + c_2\mathbf{u}_2 + c_3\mathbf{u}_3 = \frac{5}{2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} - \frac{3}{2}\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 4 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + 2\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix}$
### Orthogonal Projections
  
  **Goal:** Decompose a vector $\mathbf{y}$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$ into the sum of two vectors, one of which is a multiple of some nonzero vector $\mathbf{u}$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$ and the other orthogonal to $\mathbf{u}$.
  
  That is, we wish to write $\mathbf{y} = \hat{\mathbf{y}} + \mathbf{z}$, where $\hat{\mathbf{y}} = \alpha \mathbf{u}$ for some scalar $\alpha$ and $\mathbf{z}$ is orthogonal to $\mathbf{u}$.
### Orthogonal Projections (Continued)
  
  $\mathbf{y} - \alpha \mathbf{u} = \mathbf{z}$
  
  We need $\mathbf{z}$ to be orthogonal to $\mathbf{u}$. Thus,
  
  $0 = (\mathbf{y} - \alpha \mathbf{u}) \cdot \mathbf{u}$
  $0 = \mathbf{y} \cdot \mathbf{u} - \alpha (\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{u})$
  $\alpha = \frac{\mathbf{y} \cdot \mathbf{u}}{\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{u}}$
  
  Therefore, $\hat{\mathbf{y}} = \frac{\mathbf{y} \cdot \mathbf{u}}{\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{u}} \mathbf{u}$.
### Orthogonal Projections (Continued)
  
  If $\mathbf{y} = \hat{\mathbf{y}} + \mathbf{z}$ where $\hat{\mathbf{y}} = \alpha \mathbf{u}$, then:
  
  *   $\hat{\mathbf{y}}$ is the **orthogonal projection** of $\mathbf{y}$ onto $\mathbf{u}$.
  *   $\mathbf{z}$ is the **component** of $\mathbf{y}$ orthogonal to $\mathbf{u}$.
  
  If $L$ is the subspace spanned by $\mathbf{u}$, $\hat{\mathbf{y}}$ is also called the **orthogonal projection** of $\mathbf{y}$ onto $L$.
  
  Specifically, $\hat{\mathbf{y}} = \text{proj}_L \mathbf{y} = \frac{\mathbf{y} \cdot \mathbf{u}}{\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{u}} \mathbf{u}$.
### Example 2
  
  Let $\mathbf{y} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}$ and $\mathbf{u} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ -7 \end{bmatrix}$. Write $\mathbf{y}$ as the sum of two orthogonal vectors, one in $\text{Span } \mathbf{u}$ and one orthogonal to $\mathbf{u}$.
  
  **Solution:**
  
  1.  **Calculate the projection $\hat{\mathbf{y}}$ onto $\mathbf{u}$:**
    $\mathbf{y} \cdot \mathbf{u} = (2)(4) + (3)(-7) = 8 - 21 = -13$
    $\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{u} = (4)(4) + (-7)(-7) = 16 + 49 = 65$
    $\hat{\mathbf{y}} = \frac{\mathbf{y} \cdot \mathbf{u}}{\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{u}} \mathbf{u} = \frac{-13}{65} \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ -7 \end{bmatrix} = -\frac{1}{5} \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ -7 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -4/5 \\ 7/5 \end{bmatrix}$.
    This is the vector in $\text{Span } \mathbf{u}$.
  
  2.  **Calculate the orthogonal component $\mathbf{z}$:**
    $\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{y} - \hat{\mathbf{y}} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} -4/5 \\ 7/5 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 + 4/5 \\ 3 - 7/5 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 10/5 + 4/5 \\ 15/5 - 7/5 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 14/5 \\ 8/5 \end{bmatrix}$.
    This is the vector orthogonal to $\mathbf{u}$.
  
  3.  **Check Orthogonality (optional but good):**
    $\mathbf{z} \cdot \mathbf{u} = (14/5)(4) + (8/5)(-7) = 56/5 - 56/5 = 0$. Yes, they are orthogonal.
  
  4.  **Write the sum:**
    $\mathbf{y} = \hat{\mathbf{y}} + \mathbf{z} = \begin{bmatrix} -4/5 \\ 7/5 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 14/5 \\ 8/5 \end{bmatrix}$.
### Example 3
  
  Let $\mathbf{y} = \begin{bmatrix} -3 \\ 9 \end{bmatrix}$ and $\mathbf{u} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$. Compute the distance from $\mathbf{y}$ to the line through $\mathbf{u}$ and the origin.
  
  **Solution:**
  
  The line through $\mathbf{u}$ and the origin is $L = \text{Span}\{\mathbf{u}\}$. The distance from $\mathbf{y}$ to the line $L$ is the length of the component of $\mathbf{y}$ orthogonal to $\mathbf{u}$, which is $\lVert \mathbf{y} - \hat{\mathbf{y}} \rVert$, where $\hat{\mathbf{y}} = \text{proj}_L \mathbf{y}$.
  
  1.  **Calculate the projection $\hat{\mathbf{y}}$:**
    $\mathbf{y} \cdot \mathbf{u} = (-3)(1) + (9)(2) = -3 + 18 = 15$
    $\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{u} = (1)(1) + (2)(2) = 1 + 4 = 5$
    $\hat{\mathbf{y}} = \frac{\mathbf{y} \cdot \mathbf{u}}{\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{u}} \mathbf{u} = \frac{15}{5} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = 3 \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix}$.
  
  2.  **Calculate the orthogonal component $\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{y} - \hat{\mathbf{y}}$:**
    $\mathbf{z} = \begin{bmatrix} -3 \\ 9 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -6 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}$.
  
  3.  **Calculate the distance:**
    Distance $= \lVert \mathbf{z} \rVert = \lVert \begin{bmatrix} -6 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} \rVert = \sqrt{(-6)^2 + (3)^2} = \sqrt{36 + 9} = \sqrt{45} = \sqrt{9 \times 5} = 3\sqrt{5}$.
### Orthonormal Sets
  
  A set $\{\mathbf{u}_1, ..., \mathbf{u}_p\}$ is an **orthonormal set** if it is an orthogonal set of unit vectors.
  
  If $W = \text{Span } \{\mathbf{u}_1, ..., \mathbf{u}_p\}$, then $\{\mathbf{u}_1, ..., \mathbf{u}_p\}$ is an **orthonormal basis** for $W$.
### Matrices with Orthonormal Columns
  
  An $m \times n$ matrix $U$ has orthonormal columns if and only if $U^T U = I$.
### Matrices with Orthonormal Columns (Continued)
  
  Let $U$ be an $m \times n$ matrix with orthonormal columns, and let $\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{y}$ be vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$. Then,
  
  a. $\lVert U\mathbf{x} \rVert = \lVert \mathbf{x} \rVert$
  b. $(U\mathbf{x}) \cdot (U\mathbf{y}) = \mathbf{x} \cdot \mathbf{y}$
  c. $(U\mathbf{x}) \cdot (U\mathbf{y}) = 0$ if and only if $\mathbf{x} \cdot \mathbf{y} = 0$
### Orthogonal and Unitary Matrices
  
  An **orthogonal matrix** is a square matrix with real entries and orthonormal columns; that is, a square matrix where $U^T = U^{-1}$.
  
  A **unitary matrix** is a square matrix with complex entries and orthonormal columns.
### Example 4
  
  Determine if each statement is true or false. Assume all vectors are in $\mathbb{R}^n$.
  
  a. Every linearly independent set in $\mathbb{R}^n$ is an orthogonal set.
  b. If $\mathbf{y}$ is a linear combination of nonzero vectors from an orthogonal set, then the weights in the linear combination can be computed without row operations on a matrix.
  c. If the vectors in an orthogonal set of nonzero vectors are normalized, then some of the new vectors may not be orthogonal.
  d. A matrix with orthonormal columns is an orthogonal matrix.
  
  **Solution:**
  
  a. **False.** For example, $\left\{ \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \right\}$ is linearly independent in $\mathbb{R}^2$, but $(1)(1) + (0)(1) = 1 \neq 0$, so it's not orthogonal.
  b. **True.** This refers to the theorem about finding weights $c_j = \frac{\mathbf{y} \cdot \mathbf{u}_j}{\mathbf{u}_j \cdot \mathbf{u}_j}$ for an orthogonal basis. These formulas only involve dot products.
  c. **False.** Normalizing means dividing each vector by its length (a scalar). Let $\mathbf{u}_i, \mathbf{u}_j$ be two distinct vectors from the orthogonal set ($\mathbf{u}_i \cdot \mathbf{u}_j = 0$). The normalized vectors are $\mathbf{v}_i = \frac{1}{\|\mathbf{u}_i\|}\mathbf{u}_i$ and $\mathbf{v}_j = \frac{1}{\|\mathbf{u}_j\|}\mathbf{u}_j$. Their dot product is $\mathbf{v}_i \cdot \mathbf{v}_j = \left(\frac{1}{\|\mathbf{u}_i\|}\mathbf{u}_i\right) \cdot \left(\frac{1}{\|\mathbf{u}_j\|}\mathbf{u}_j\right) = \frac{1}{\|\mathbf{u}_i\|\|\mathbf{u}_j\|} (\mathbf{u}_i \cdot \mathbf{u}_j) = \frac{1}{\|\mathbf{u}_i\|\|\mathbf{u}_j\|} (0) = 0$. The normalized set remains orthogonal.
  d. **False.** An orthogonal matrix must be *square*. A non-square matrix can have orthonormal columns (e.g., $U = \begin{bmatrix} 1/\sqrt{2} & 0 \\ 1/\sqrt{2} & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$), but it's not called an orthogonal matrix.
### Example 5
  
  Suppose $W$ is a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^n$ spanned by $n$ nonzero orthogonal vectors. Explain why $W = \mathbb{R}^n$.
  
  **Solution:**
  
  Let the set of $n$ nonzero orthogonal vectors be $S = \{\mathbf{v}_1, ..., \mathbf{v}_n\}$.
  1.  Since the vectors in $S$ are orthogonal and nonzero, they are linearly independent (by the theorem stated earlier in this section).
  2.  We have a set of $n$ linearly independent vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$.
  3.  Any set of $n$ linearly independent vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$ forms a basis for $\mathbb{R}^n$.
  4.  Therefore, $S$ is a basis for $\mathbb{R}^n$.
  5.  The subspace spanned by $S$ is $W = \text{Span}\{\mathbf{v}_1, ..., \mathbf{v}_n\}$.
  6.  Since $S$ is a basis for $\mathbb{R}^n$, the span of $S$ is $\mathbb{R}^n$.
  7.  Thus, $W = \mathbb{R}^n$.
