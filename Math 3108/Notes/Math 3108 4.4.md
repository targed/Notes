## Section 4.4: Coordinate Systems
### Coordinates
  
  Suppose $\mathcal{B} = \{\mathbf{b}_1, ..., \mathbf{b}_n\}$ is a basis for a vector space $V$ and $\mathbf{x}$ is a vector in $V$. The **coordinates of** $\mathbf{x}$ **relative to the basis** $\mathcal{B}$ (or the $\mathcal{B}$**-coordinates of** $\mathbf{x}$) are the weights $c_1, ..., c_n$ such that:
  
  $\mathbf{x} = c_1\mathbf{b}_1 + ... + c_n\mathbf{b}_n$
  
  The $\mathcal{B}$-coordinate vector of $\mathbf{x}$ is:
  
  $[\mathbf{x}]_\mathcal{B} = \begin{bmatrix} c_1 \\ \vdots \\ c_n \end{bmatrix}$
### Example 1
  
  The sets $\mathcal{B}_1 = \{1, t, t^2\}$ and $\mathcal{B}_2 = \{1, 1+t, 1+t+t^2\}$ are both bases for $\mathbb{P}_2$. Find the coordinate vectors for $\mathbf{p}(t) = 4 - 6t + 3t^2$ under the basis $\mathcal{B}_1$ and under the basis $\mathcal{B}_2$.
  
  **Solution:**
  
  *   **Basis $\mathcal{B}_1$:**
  
    The coordinates of $\mathbf{p}(t) = 4 - 6t + 3t^2$ with respect to $\mathcal{B}_1 = \{1, t, t^2\}$ are simply the coefficients of the polynomial:
  
    $[\mathbf{p}]_{\mathcal{B}_1} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ -6 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}$
  
  *   **Basis $\mathcal{B}_2$:**
  
    We need to find scalars $c_1$, $c_2$, and $c_3$ such that:
  
    $c_1(1) + c_2(1+t) + c_3(1+t+t^2) = 4 - 6t + 3t^2$
  
    Expanding and collecting like terms:
  
    $(c_1 + c_2 + c_3) + (c_2 + c_3)t + c_3t^2 = 4 - 6t + 3t^2$
  
    Equating coefficients:
  
    $c_1 + c_2 + c_3 = 4$
    $c_2 + c_3 = -6$
    $c_3 = 3$
  
    Solving this system:
    $c_3 = 3$
    $c_2 = -6 - c_3 = -6 - 3 = -9$
    $c_1 = 4 - c_2 - c_3 = 4 - (-9) - 3 = 10$
  
    Therefore, $[\mathbf{p}]_{\mathcal{B}_2} = \begin{bmatrix} 10 \\ -9 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}$
### Example 2
  
  The set $\mathcal{B} = \left\{ \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 6 \\ 7 \end{bmatrix} \right\}$ is a basis for $\mathbb{R}^2$. Find the vector $\mathbf{x}$ determined by the $\mathcal{B}$-coordinate vector $[\mathbf{x}]_\mathcal{B} = \begin{bmatrix} 8 \\ -5 \end{bmatrix}$.
  
  **Solution:**
  
  The vector $\mathbf{x}$ is a linear combination of the basis vectors in $\mathcal{B}$, with the weights given by the entries of $[\mathbf{x}]_\mathcal{B}$:
  
  $\mathbf{x} = 8\begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix} + (-5)\begin{bmatrix} 6 \\ 7 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 32 \\ 40 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} -30 \\ -35 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix}$
### The Unique Representation Theorem
  
  Let $\mathcal{B} = \{\mathbf{b}_1, ..., \mathbf{b}_n\}$ be a basis for a vector space $V$. Then, for each $\mathbf{x}$ in $V$, there exists a unique set of scalars $c_1, ..., c_n$ such that:
  
  $\mathbf{x} = c_1\mathbf{b}_1 + ... + c_n\mathbf{b}_n$
### Example 3
  Prove unique representation theorem
  **Solution:**
  We know that because B is a basis, there must exist some scalars such that $\mathbf{x} = c_1\mathbf{b}_1 + ... + c_n\mathbf{b}_n$
  We must only now prove that there is a unique combination of scalars to satisfy that equation.
  Suppose there were some other vector with different scalars:
  $\mathbf{x} = d_1\mathbf{b}_1 + ... + d_n\mathbf{b}_n$
  
  We then can say that:
  $c_1\mathbf{b}_1 + ... + c_n\mathbf{b}_n = d_1\mathbf{b}_1 + ... + d_n\mathbf{b}_n$
  
  Rearranged:
  $(c_1 - d_1)\mathbf{b}_1 + ... + (c_n - d_n)\mathbf{b}_n = 0$
  
  However, since B is a basis, we know the vectors must be linearly independent. This means that all the coefficients must be 0.
  
  $(c_1 - d_1) = 0 ... (c_n - d_n) = 0$
  $c_1 = d_1 ... c_n = d_n$
  
  This means the scalars can not be different, so the representation must be unique.
### Example 4
  
  The set $\mathcal{B} = \left\{ \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 6 \\ 7 \end{bmatrix} \right\}$ is a basis for $\mathbb{R}^2$. Find the coordinate vector $[\mathbf{x}]_\mathcal{B}$ of $\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}$.
  
  **Solution:**
  
  We need to find scalars $c_1$ and $c_2$ such that:
  
  $c_1 \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix} + c_2 \begin{bmatrix} 6 \\ 7 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}$
  
  This is equivalent to solving the system:
  
  $4c_1 + 6c_2 = 2$
  $5c_1 + 7c_2 = 3$
  
  We can solve this system using various methods (substitution, elimination, or matrix methods).  Here's the solution using an augmented matrix:
  
  $\begin{bmatrix} 4 & 6 & 2 \\ 5 & 7 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \sim \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3/2 & 1/2 \\ 5 & 7 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \sim \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3/2 & 1/2 \\ 0 & -1/2 & 1/2 \end{bmatrix} \sim \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3/2 & 1/2 \\ 0 & 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix} \sim \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$
  
  So, $c_1 = 2$ and $c_2 = -1$.
  
  Therefore, $[\mathbf{x}]_\mathcal{B} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}$.
### Change of Coordinates Matrices
  
  To change coordinates from a basis $\mathcal{B}$ to the standard basis in $\mathbb{R}^n$, we can compute:
  
  $\mathbf{x} = P_\mathcal{B}[\mathbf{x}]_\mathcal{B}$
  
  for some matrix $P_\mathcal{B}$. In general, we call $P_\mathcal{B}$ the **change of coordinates matrix** from $\mathcal{B}$ to the standard basis in $\mathbb{R}^n$.
  
  Similarly, we can go the other direction using the inverse of $P_\mathcal{B}$:
  
  $P_\mathcal{B}^{-1}\mathbf{x} = [\mathbf{x}]_\mathcal{B}$
### Coordinate Mappings
  
  Let $\mathcal{B} = \{\mathbf{b}_1, ..., \mathbf{b}_n\}$ be a basis for a vector space $V$. This vector space $V$ might not be $\mathbb{R}^n$.
  
  Then, the coordinate mapping $\mathbf{x} \mapsto [\mathbf{x}]_\mathcal{B}$ is a one-to-one linear transformation from $V$ onto $\mathbb{R}^n$.
### Isomorphisms
  
  A one-to-one linear transformation from a vector space $V$ onto a vector space $W$ is called an **isomorphism** from $V$ onto $W$.
  
  If there exists an isomorphism from $V$ onto $W$, then $V$ and $W$ are said to be **isomorphic**.
  
  Two isomorphic vector spaces have the same general structure, and every calculation in one space is accurately reproduced in the other.
  
  The coordinate mapping $\mathbf{x} \mapsto [\mathbf{x}]_\mathcal{B}$ is an isomorphism.
### Example 5
  
  Recall that $\mathcal{B} = \{1, t, t^2, t^3\}$ is the standard basis for the vector space $\mathbb{P}_3$.
  
  Elements in $\mathbb{P}_3$ are of the form $\mathbf{p}(t) = a_0 + a_1t + a_2t^2 + a_3t^3$.
  
  Thus, the coordinate vector of $\mathbf{p}$ relative to $\mathcal{B}$ is $[\mathbf{p}]_\mathcal{B} = \begin{bmatrix} a_0 \\ a_1 \\ a_2 \\ a_3 \end{bmatrix}$.
  
  This is an isomorphism from $\mathbb{P}_3$ onto $\mathbb{R}^4$.
  
  **Explanation (No additional steps needed):**  This example illustrates the concept of an isomorphism. The coordinate mapping takes a polynomial in $\mathbb{P}_3$ and maps it to a vector in $\mathbb{R}^4$ that represents the coefficients of the polynomial.  This mapping preserves the vector space structure (addition and scalar multiplication).
### Example 6
  
  Use coordinate vectors to test the linear independence of the set of polynomials.
  
  $\{1 + 2t^3, 2 + t - 3t^2, -t + 2t^2 - t^3\}$
  
  Do these polynomials form a basis for $\mathbb{P}_3$?
  
  **Solution:**
  
  1.  **Find the coordinate vectors:**  We use the standard basis for $\mathbb{P}_3$, which is $\{1, t, t^2, t^3\}$. The coordinate vectors are:
  
    *   $[1 + 2t^3]_\mathcal{B} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$
    *   $[2 + t - 3t^2]_\mathcal{B} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \\ -3 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$
    *   $[-t + 2t^2 - t^3]_\mathcal{B} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -1 \\ 2 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}$
  
  2.  **Form a matrix with the coordinate vectors as columns:**
  
    $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & -1 \\ 0 & -3 & 2 \\ 2 & 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$
  
  3.  **Check for linear independence:** We can row reduce the matrix to determine if the columns are linearly independent.
  
    $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & -1 \\ 0 & -3 & 2 \\ 2 & 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix} \sim \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & -1 \\ 0 & -3 & 2 \\ 0 & -4 & -1 \end{bmatrix} \sim \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & -5 \end{bmatrix} \sim \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$
  
  There are three pivot columns. This indicates the column vectors are linearly independent.
  
  4.  **Basis for P3?**
  The vectors are linearly independent. However, there are only 3 of them. P3 has dimension 4, meaning the set of vectors cannot form a basis, because you need 4 linearly independent vectors to form a basis.
