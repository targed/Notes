## Section 2.2: The Inverse of a Matrix

### The Determinant of a 2 x 2 Matrix
  
  Let $A = \begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix}$. The determinant of $A$ is:
  
  $\text{det } A = \begin{vmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{vmatrix} = ad - bc$
### Example 1
  
  Calculate the determinant of $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$.
  
  **Solution:**
  
  $\text{det } A = (1)(4) - (2)(3) = 4 - 6 = -2$
### The Inverse of a Matrix
  
  An $n \times n$ matrix $A$ is said to be **invertible** if there is an $n \times n$ matrix $C$ such that:
  
  $CA = I_n$ and $AC = I_n$
  
  where $I_n$ is the $n \times n$ identity matrix.
  
  $C$ is the **inverse** of $A$, and we usually write $C = A^{-1}$.
  
  *   An invertible matrix is called **nonsingular**.
  *   A noninvertible matrix is called **singular**.
### Example 2
  
  Compute the products $CA$ and $AC$ given:
  
  $A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 2 \\ 7 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$ and $C = \begin{bmatrix} -2 & 1 \\ \frac{7}{2} & -\frac{3}{2} \end{bmatrix}$
  
  **Solution:**
  
  $CA = \begin{bmatrix} -2 & 1 \\ \frac{7}{2} & -\frac{3}{2} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 2 \\ 7 & 4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} (-2)(3) + (1)(7) & (-2)(2) + (1)(4) \\ (\frac{7}{2})(3) + (-\frac{3}{2})(7) & (\frac{7}{2})(2) + (-\frac{3}{2})(4) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$
  
  $AC =  \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 2 \\ 7 & 4 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} -2 & 1 \\ \frac{7}{2} & -\frac{3}{2} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} (3)(-2) + (2)(\frac{7}{2}) & (3)(1) + (2)(-\frac{3}{2}) \\ (7)(-2) + (4)(\frac{7}{2}) & (7)(1) + (4)(-\frac{3}{2}) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$
  
  Since $CA = AC = I$, $C$ is the inverse of $A$.
### The Inverse of a 2 x 2 Matrix
  
  Let $A = \begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix}$.
  
  If $\text{det } A \neq 0$, then $A$ is invertible, and:
  
  $A^{-1} = \frac{1}{\text{det } A} \begin{bmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{bmatrix}$
  
  If $\text{det } A = 0$, then $A$ is not invertible.
### Example 3
  
  Calculate the inverse of $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$.
  
  **Solution:**
  
  1.  **Calculate the determinant:** $\text{det } A = (1)(4) - (2)(3) = 4 - 6 = -2$
  
  2.  **Since the determinant is nonzero, the inverse exists.**
  
  3.  **Apply the formula:** $A^{-1} = \frac{1}{-2} \begin{bmatrix} 4 & -2 \\ -3 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -2 & 1 \\ \frac{3}{2} & -\frac{1}{2} \end{bmatrix}$
### Solving Systems Using Inverses
  
  If $A$ is an invertible $n \times n$ matrix, then for each $\mathbf{b}$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$, the equation $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ has the unique solution $\mathbf{x} = A^{-1}\mathbf{b}$.
### Example 4
  
  Solve the system:
  
  $x + 2y = 5$
  $3x + 4y = 6$
  
  **Solution:**
  
  1.  **Write the system in matrix form:** $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix}$
  
  2.  **The coefficient matrix is the same as in Example 3.**  We found $A^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} -2 & 1 \\ \frac{3}{2} & -\frac{1}{2} \end{bmatrix}$
  
  3.  **Multiply both sides by  $A^{-1}$:**  $\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix} = A^{-1} \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -2 & 1 \\ \frac{3}{2} & -\frac{1}{2} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} (-2)(5) + (1)(6) \\ (\frac{3}{2})(5) + (-\frac{1}{2})(6) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -4 \\ \frac{9}{2} \end{bmatrix}$
  
  4.  **Solution:** $x = -4$, $y = \frac{9}{2}$
### Facts About Invertibility
  
  *   If $A$ is an invertible matrix, then $A^{-1}$ is invertible and $(A^{-1})^{-1} = A$.
  *   If $A$ and $B$ are invertible $n \times n$ matrices, then so is $AB$; specifically, $(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}$.
  *   The product of invertible $n \times n$ matrices is invertible, and their inverse is the product of their inverses in the reverse order.
  *   If $A$ is an invertible matrix, then so is $A^T$, and $(A^T)^{-1} = (A^{-1})^T$.
### Example 5
  
  Prove that $(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}$ by calculating $(AB)(B^{-1}A^{-1})$ and $(B^{-1}A^{-1})(AB)$.
  
  **Solution:**
  $(AB)(B^{-1}A^{-1}) = A(BB^{-1})A^{-1} = AIA^{-1} = AA^{-1} = I$
  $(B^{-1}A^{-1})(AB) = B^{-1}(A^{-1}A)B = B^{-1}IB = B^{-1}B = I$
  Since both products result in the identity matrix, $(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}$.
### Example 6
  
  Suppose $A$, $B$, and $C$ are invertible $n \times n$ matrices. Show that $ABC$ is also invertible by producing a matrix $D$ such that $(ABC)D = I$ and $D(ABC) = I$.
  
  **Solution:**
  Let $D = C^{-1}B^{-1}A^{-1}$.
  $(ABC)D = (ABC)(C^{-1}B^{-1}A^{-1}) = A(BB^{-1})(CC^{-1})A^{-1}= AIA^{-1}=AA^{-1} = I$
  $D(ABC) = (C^{-1}B^{-1}A^{-1})(ABC) = C^{-1}(B^{-1}B)(A^{-1}A)C = C^{-1}IC = C^{-1}C=I$
  Since the products in both orders give us $I$, then we know $D$ is the inverse of $ABC$ which means that $ABC$ is invertible.
### Elementary Matrices
  
  An **elementary matrix** is a matrix obtained by performing a single elementary row operation on an identity matrix.
### Example 7
  
  Let $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \\ 7 & 8 & 10 \end{bmatrix}$.
  
  Perform the replacement operation $-4R_1 + R_2 \rightarrow R_2$ on $A$ to get $B$ and on $I_3$ to get $E$.
  
  Then, multiply $EA$.
  **Solution:**
  Applying $-4R_1 + R_2 \rightarrow R_2$ to $A$:
  $B= \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 0 & -3 & -6 \\ 7 & 8 & 10 \end{bmatrix}$.
  
  Applying $-4R_1 + R_2 \rightarrow R_2$ to $I_3$ gives us the Elementary matrix:
  $E = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -4 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$
  
  $EA = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -4 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \\ 7 & 8 & 10 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 0 & -3 & -6 \\ 7 & 8 & 10 \end{bmatrix} = B$
### Elementary Matrices (Continued)
  
  *   If an elementary row operation is performed on an $m \times n$ matrix $A$, the resulting matrix can be written as $EA$, where the $m \times m$ matrix $E$ is created by performing the same row operation on $I_m$.
  *   Each elementary matrix $E$ is invertible, and the inverse of $E$ is itself an elementary matrix.
### Theorem
  
  An $n \times n$ matrix $A$ is invertible if and only if $A$ is row equivalent to $I_n$, and in this case, any sequence of elementary row operations that reduces $A$ to $I_n$ also transforms $I_n$ into $A^{-1}$.
### Example 8
  
  Prove this theorem.
  We can prove this through the use of elementary matrices.
  If A is invertible, it is row equivalent to $I_n$
  $E_p...E_1A = I$
  Therefore, we can multiply both sides by the inverses of $E_1, E_2 ... E_p$. This reduces the left to just A, while the right gives us a matrix which we will call B.
  $A = E_1^{-1}E_2^{-1}...E_p^{-1}I$
  $A = B$
  Now lets multiply both sides by $A^{-1}$.
  $AA^{-1} = BA^{-1} -> I = BA^{-1}$
  This means the same series of row operations to transform A to the identity matrix will also transform the identity to A inverse.
### Algorithm for Finding $A^{-1}$
  
  To find $A^{-1}$, row reduce the augmented matrix $\left[ \begin{array}{c|c} A & I \end{array} \right]$.
  
  If $A$ is row equivalent to $I$ (and thus $A$ is invertible), then $\left[ \begin{array}{c|c} A & I \end{array} \right]$ is row equivalent to $\left[ \begin{array}{c|c} I & A^{-1} \end{array} \right]$.
### Example 9
  
  Find the inverse of $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \\ 7 & 8 & 10 \end{bmatrix}$.
  
  **Solution:**
  
  1.  **Form the augmented matrix:** $\left[ \begin{array}{ccc|ccc} 1 & 2 & 3 & 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 & 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 7 & 8 & 10 & 0 & 0 & 1 \end{array} \right]$
  
  2.  **Row reduce:**
  
    *   $R_2 \rightarrow R_2 - 4R_1$: $\left[ \begin{array}{ccc|ccc} 1 & 2 & 3 & 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & -3 & -6 & -4 & 1 & 0 \\ 7 & 8 & 10 & 0 & 0 & 1 \end{array} \right]$
    *   $R_3 \rightarrow R_3 - 7R_1$: $\left[ \begin{array}{ccc|ccc} 1 & 2 & 3 & 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & -3 & -6 & -4 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -6 & -11 & -7 & 0 & 1 \end{array} \right]$
    *   $R_2 \rightarrow -\frac{1}{3}R_2$: $\left[ \begin{array}{ccc|ccc} 1 & 2 & 3 & 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 2 & \frac{4}{3} & -\frac{1}{3} & 0 \\ 0 & -6 & -11 & -7 & 0 & 1 \end{array} \right]$
    *   $R_3 \rightarrow R_3 + 6R_2$: $\left[ \begin{array}{ccc|ccc} 1 & 2 & 3 & 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 2 & \frac{4}{3} & -\frac{1}{3} & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 1 & -2 & 1 \end{array} \right]$
    *   $R_1 \rightarrow R_1 - 3R_3$: $\left[ \begin{array}{ccc|ccc} 1 & 2 & 0 & -2 & 6 & -3 \\ 0 & 1 & 2 & \frac{4}{3} & -\frac{1}{3} & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 1 & -2 & 1 \end{array} \right]$
    *   $R_2 \rightarrow R_2 - 2R_3$: $\left[ \begin{array}{ccc|ccc} 1 & 2 & 0 & -2 & 6 & -3 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 & -\frac{2}{3} & \frac{11}{3} & -2 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 1 & -2 & 1 \end{array} \right]$
    *  $R_1 \rightarrow R_1 - 2R_2$:  $\left[ \begin{array}{ccc|ccc} 1 & 0 & 0 & -\frac{2}{3} & -\frac{4}{3} & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 & -\frac{2}{3} & \frac{11}{3} & -2 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 1 & -2 & 1 \end{array} \right]$
  
  3. **Result:** $A^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} -\frac{2}{3} & -\frac{4}{3} & 1 \\ -\frac{2}{3} & \frac{11}{3} & -2 \\ 1 & -2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$m
