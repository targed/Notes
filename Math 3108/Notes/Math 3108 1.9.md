## Section 1.9: The Matrix of a Linear Transformation
### Identity Matrices
  
  *   **2 x 2 identity matrix:**
    $I_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$. Its columns are $\mathbf{e}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$ and $\mathbf{e}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$.
  *   **3 x 3 identity matrix:**
    $I_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$. Its columns are $\mathbf{e}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$, $\mathbf{e}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$, and $\mathbf{e}_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$.
  *   **n x n identity matrix:**
    $I_n = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & ... & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & ... & 0 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & ... & 1 \end{bmatrix}$. Its columns are $\mathbf{e}_1, \mathbf{e}_2, ..., \mathbf{e}_n$, where $\mathbf{e}_j$ is a vector with a 1 in the $j$th position and 0s elsewhere.
  
  When the size is clear from the context, we often refer to the identity matrix as simply $I$.
### Theorem
  
  If $T: \mathbb{R}^n \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^m$ is a linear transformation, then there exists a unique matrix $A$ such that $T(\mathbf{x}) = A\mathbf{x}$ for all $\mathbf{x}$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$.
  
  Specifically, $A$ is the $m \times n$ matrix $A = \begin{bmatrix} T(\mathbf{e}_1) & ... & T(\mathbf{e}_n) \end{bmatrix}$, where $\mathbf{e}_j$ is the $j$th column of the $n \times n$ identity matrix.
  
  $A$ is called the **standard matrix** for the linear transformation $T$.

### Example 2

### Common Transformations
  
  The following tables show some common geometric transformations in $\mathbb{R}^2$ and their standard matrices:
  
  **TABLE 1: Reflections**
  
  | Transformation                                     | Image of the Unit Square                                                                                                             | Standard Matrix                                  |
  | :------------------------------------------------- | :------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | :----------------------------------------------- |
  | Reflection through the $x_1$-axis                 | A square with a corner at (0,0), (1,0), (0,-1), (1,-1)                                                                                | $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$ |
  | Reflection through the $x_2$-axis                 | A square with a corner at (0,0), (-1,0), (0,1), (-1,1)                                                                               | $\begin{bmatrix} -1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ |
  | Reflection through the line $x_2 = x_1$           | A square with a corner at (0,0), (0,1), (1,0), (1,1)                                                                                 | $\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ |
  | Reflection through the line $x_2 = -x_1$          | A square with a corner at (0,0), (0,-1), (-1,0), (-1,-1)                                                                              | $\begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ |
  | Reflection through the origin                     | A square with a corner at (0,0), (-1,0), (0,-1), (-1,-1)                                                                              | $\begin{bmatrix} -1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$ |
  
  **TABLE 2: Contractions and Expansions**
  
  | Transformation             | Image of the Unit Square                                                             | Standard Matrix                                  |
  | :------------------------- | :----------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | :----------------------------------------------- |
  | Horizontal contraction      | A rectangle with a corner at (0,0), (k,0), (0,1), (k,1), where 0 < k < 1               | $\begin{bmatrix} k & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ |
  | Horizontal expansion       | A rectangle with a corner at (0,0), (k,0), (0,1), (k,1), where k > 1               | $\begin{bmatrix} k & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ |
  | Vertical contraction        | A rectangle with a corner at (0,0), (1,0), (0,k), (1,k), where 0 < k < 1               | $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & k \end{bmatrix}$ |
  | Vertical expansion         | A rectangle with a corner at (0,0), (1,0), (0,k), (1,k), where k > 1               | $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & k \end{bmatrix}$ |
  
  **TABLE 3: Shears**
  
  | Transformation    | Image of the Unit Square                                                              | Standard Matrix                                  |
  | :---------------- | :------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | :----------------------------------------------- |
  | Horizontal shear  | A parallelogram with a corner at (0,0), (1,0), (k,1), (k+1,1)                            | $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & k \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ |
  | Vertical shear    | A parallelogram with a corner at (0,0), (1,k), (0,1), (1,k+1)                           | $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ k & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ |
  
  **TABLE 4: Projections**
  
  | Transformation                | Image of the Unit Square                                              | Standard Matrix                                  |
  | :---------------------------- | :-------------------------------------------------------------------- | :----------------------------------------------- |
  | Projection onto the $x_1$-axis | A line segment with endpoints at (0,0) and (1,0)                         | $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ |
  | Projection onto the $x_2$-axis | A line segment with endpoints at (0,0) and (0,1)                        | $\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ |
### One-to-One and Onto Transformations
  
  *   A mapping $T: \mathbb{R}^n \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^m$ is said to be **onto (surjective)** $\mathbb{R}^m$ if each $\mathbf{b}$ in $\mathbb{R}^m$ is the image of at least one $\mathbf{x}$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$. Equivalently, $T$ is onto if its range is equal to its entire codomain.
  *   A mapping $T: \mathbb{R}^n \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^m$ is said to be **one-to-one (injective)** if each $\mathbf{b}$ in $\mathbb{R}^m$ is the image of *at most* one $\mathbf{x}$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$. Equivalently, $T$ is one-to-one if, for each $\mathbf{b}$ in $\mathbb{R}^m$, the equation $T(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{b}$ has either a unique solution or none at all.
### Example 3
  
  Let $T$ be the linear transformation whose standard matrix is
  
  $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -4 & 8 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 & -1 & 3 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 5 \end{bmatrix}$
  
  Does $T$ map $\mathbb{R}^4$ onto $\mathbb{R}^3$? Is $T$ a one-to-one mapping?
### Theorem
  
  Let $T: \mathbb{R}^n \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^m$ be a linear transformation. Then, $T$ is one-to-one if and only if the equation $T(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{0}$ has only the trivial solution.
### Theorem
  
  Let $T: \mathbb{R}^n \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^m$ be a linear transformation, and let $A$ be the standard matrix for $T$. Then:
  
  a. $T$ maps $\mathbb{R}^n$ onto $\mathbb{R}^m$ if and only if the columns of $A$ span $\mathbb{R}^m$ (which occurs if and only if $A$ has a pivot in every row).
  b. $T$ is one-to-one if and only if the columns of $A$ are linearly independent (which occurs if and only if $A$ has a pivot in every column).
