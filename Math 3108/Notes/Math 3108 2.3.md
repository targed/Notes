## Section 2.3: Characterizations of Invertible Matrices
### The Invertible Matrix Theorem
  
  Let $A$ be a square $n \times n$ matrix. Then the following statements are equivalent (i.e., they are either all true or all false):
  
  a. $A$ is an invertible matrix.
  b. $A$ is row equivalent to $I_n$.
  c. $A$ has $n$ pivot positions.
  d. The equation $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$ has only the trivial solution.
  e. The columns of $A$ form a linearly independent set.
  f. The linear transformation $\mathbf{x} \mapsto A\mathbf{x}$ is one-to-one.
  g. The equation $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ has at least one solution for each $\mathbf{b}$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$.
  h. The columns of $A$ span $\mathbb{R}^n$.
  i. The linear transformation $\mathbf{x} \mapsto A\mathbf{x}$ maps $\mathbb{R}^n$ onto $\mathbb{R}^n$.
  j. There is an $n \times n$ matrix $C$ such that $CA = I$.
  k. There is an $n \times n$ matrix $D$ such that $AD = I$.
  l. $A^T$ is an invertible matrix.
### Fact
  
  Let $A$ and $B$ be square $n \times n$ matrices. If $AB = I$, then $A$ and $B$ are both invertible, with $B = A^{-1}$ and $A = B^{-1}$.
### Comments
  
  *   Each statement in the Invertible Matrix Theorem describes a property of every $n \times n$ invertible matrix.
  *   The negation of a statement in the theorem describes a property of every $n \times n$ singular (noninvertible) matrix.
### Example 1
  
  Determine if each matrix is invertible:
  
  $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 2 & 0 & 0 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \end{bmatrix}$,
  $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 2 & 3 & 0 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \end{bmatrix}$
  
  **Solution:**
  
  *   **Matrix 1:** $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 2 & 0 & 0 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \end{bmatrix}$
  
    This matrix has a column of zeros (the second column).  Therefore, its columns are linearly *dependent*.  By the Invertible Matrix Theorem (statement e), this matrix is **not invertible**.
  
  *   **Matrix 2:** $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 2 & 3 & 0 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \end{bmatrix}$
  
    This matrix is lower triangular.  Its determinant is the product of the diagonal entries: $(1)(3)(6) = 18$. Since the determinant is nonzero, the matrix is **invertible** (by the Invertible Matrix Theorem, and a later addition stating det A must not equal 0).
### Example 2
  
  If $C$ is $6 \times 6$ and the equation $C\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{v}$ is consistent for every $\mathbf{v}$ in $\mathbb{R}^6$, is it possible that for some $\mathbf{v}$, the equation $C\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{v}$ has more than one solution? Why or why not?
  
  **Solution:**
  
  If $C\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{v}$ is consistent for *every* $\mathbf{v}$ in $\mathbb{R}^6$, this means the columns of $C$ span $\mathbb{R}^6$ (Invertible Matrix Theorem, statement h).  Since $C$ is a $6 \times 6$ matrix, and its columns span $\mathbb{R}^6$, $C$ has 6 pivot positions (statement c). Consequently, the equation $C\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{v}$ will have a solution, and because it has a pivot in every column it means that it must be one to one.
  
  Therefore, $C$ is invertible. By the Invertible Matrix Theorem, the equation $C\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{v}$ has a *unique* solution for every $\mathbf{v}$. So, it is **not possible** for the equation to have more than one solution.
### Example 3
  
  If $A$ is an $n \times n$ matrix, then the equation $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ has at least one solution for each $\mathbf{b}$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$.
  
  This statement by itself does *not* guarantee that A is invertible and therefore is not necessarily true, meaning the prompt is a false statement.
  
  **Solution/Explanation:**
  
  This statement is part of the Invertible Matrix Theorem (statement g). However, the theorem states that *all* the statements are equivalent *if and only if* $A$ is invertible.  The statement, taken on its own, does *not* imply that $A$ is invertible.  It's possible to have a non-square matrix where $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ has a solution for every $\mathbf{b}$, but such a matrix is not invertible.
  
  For example, consider $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$.  For any $\mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} b_1 \\ b_2 \\ b_3 \end{bmatrix}$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$, we can see if there's a solution to $A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}$. However, if $b_3$ is anything but 0, there is not solution. Therefore, this statement is not always true.
### Example 4
  
  Show that if $AB$ is invertible, so is $B$.
  
  **Solution:**
  
  If $AB$ is invertible, there exists a matrix $W$ such that $(AB)W = I$ and $W(AB) = I$.
  
  We want to show that $B$ is invertible.  Let's look at the equation $(AB)W = I$.  We can rewrite this as $A(BW) = I$.
  
  Let $C = BW$. Then we have $AC = I$.  By the Invertible Matrix Theorem (statement j), if there exists a matrix $C$ such that $AC=I$, then $A$ is invertible.
  
  Now consider $W(AB)=I$, and rearrange using associativity:
  $(WA)B = I$
  Call $WA = D$.
  Therefore $DB = I$.
  Since we have shown a matrix D such that DB = I, then we know via the invertible matrix theorem that B must also be invertible.

### Invertible Linear Transformations
  
  A linear transformation $T: \mathbb{R}^n \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^n$ is **invertible** if there exists a function $S: \mathbb{R}^n \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^n$ such that:
  
  *   $S(T(\mathbf{x})) = \mathbf{x}$ for all $\mathbf{x}$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$
  *   $T(S(\mathbf{x})) = \mathbf{x}$ for all $\mathbf{x}$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$
### Theorem
  
  Let $T: \mathbb{R}^n \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^n$ be a linear transformation and let $A$ be the standard matrix for $T$. Then, $T$ is invertible if and only if $A$ is an invertible matrix. In that case, $S(\mathbf{x}) = A^{-1}\mathbf{x}$ satisfies the conditions for being the inverse transformation.
### Example 5
  
  Show that $T: \mathbb{R}^2 \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^2$ is invertible and find a formula for $T^{-1}$, where $T(x_1, x_2) = (-5x_1 + 9x_2, 4x_1 - 7x_2)$.
  
  **Steps:**
  1. Create standard matrix A.
  $A = \begin{bmatrix} -5 & 9 \\ 4 & -7 \end{bmatrix}$
  2. Using a method to solve for an inverse, find the inverse of A to be:
  $A^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} -7 & -9 \\ -4 & -5 \end{bmatrix}$
  3. Therefore the inverse of T is equal to:
  $T^{-1}(x_1, x_2) = (-7x_1 -9x_2, -4x_1 -5x_2)$
