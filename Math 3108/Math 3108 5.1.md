## Section 5.1: Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors
### Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors
  
  An **eigenvector** of an $n \times n$ matrix $A$ is a nonzero vector $\mathbf{x}$ such that $A\mathbf{x} = \lambda \mathbf{x}$ for some scalar $\lambda$.
  
  A scalar $\lambda$ is called an **eigenvalue** of $A$ if there is a nontrivial solution $\mathbf{x}$ of $A\mathbf{x} = \lambda \mathbf{x}$. Such an $\mathbf{x}$ is called an **eigenvector** of $A$ corresponding to $\lambda$.
### Example 1
  
  Is $\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$ an eigenvector of $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$? If so, find the corresponding eigenvalue.
  
  **Solution:**
  
  1.  **Compute $A\mathbf{x}$:** Let $\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$.
    $A\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} (2)(-1) + (1)(1) \\ (1)(-1) + (4)(1) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -2 + 1 \\ -1 + 4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}$
  
  2.  **Check if $A\mathbf{x}$ is a scalar multiple of $\mathbf{x}$:** We need to see if there is a scalar $\lambda$ such that $A\mathbf{x} = \lambda \mathbf{x}$.
    $\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} = \lambda \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$
    This equation implies $-1 = \lambda(-1)$ and $3 = \lambda(1)$. The first equation gives $\lambda = 1$, while the second gives $\lambda = 3$. Since we cannot find a single scalar $\lambda$ that satisfies the equation, $\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$ is **not** an eigenvector of $A$.

### Example 2
  
  Is $\lambda = 2$ an eigenvalue of $A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 3 \\ 2 & 8 \end{bmatrix}$?
  
  **Solution:**
  
  $\lambda = 2$ is an eigenvalue if and only if the equation $(A - \lambda I)\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$ has a nontrivial solution.
  
  1.  **Form $A - \lambda I$:**
    $A - 2I = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 3 \\ 2 & 8 \end{bmatrix} - 2\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 3 \\ 2 & 8 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 6 \end{bmatrix}$
  
  2.  **Check if $(A - 2I)\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$ has a nontrivial solution:**
    Consider the augmented matrix $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 & 0 \\ 2 & 6 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$.
    Row reduce: $R_2 \rightarrow R_2 - 2R_1$
    $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$
    This corresponds to the equation $x_1 + 3x_2 = 0$. There is a free variable ($x_2$), so there are nontrivial solutions (e.g., $x_2=1, x_1=-3$).
  
  3.  **Conclusion:** Yes, $\lambda = 2$ is an eigenvalue of $A$.
### Eigenspaces
  
  The equation $A\mathbf{x} = \lambda \mathbf{x}$ is equivalent to $(A - \lambda I)\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$.
  
  The set of all solutions of this equation is the null space of $A - \lambda I$, and this set is called the **eigenspace** of $A$ corresponding to $\lambda$.
### Example 3
  
  Find a basis for the eigenspace of $A = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 2 & 3 \\ -1 & 1 & -3 \\ 2 & 4 & 9 \end{bmatrix}$ corresponding to $\lambda = 3$.
  
  **Solution:**
  
  1.  **Form $A - \lambda I$:**
    $A - 3I = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 2 & 3 \\ -1 & 1 & -3 \\ 2 & 4 & 9 \end{bmatrix} - 3\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ -1 & -2 & -3 \\ 2 & 4 & 6 \end{bmatrix}$
  
  2.  **Solve $(A - 3I)\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$:**
    Form the augmented matrix and row reduce:
    $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 & 0 \\ -1 & -2 & -3 & 0 \\ 2 & 4 & 6 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{R_2 \to R_2 + R_1, R_3 \to R_3 - 2R_1} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$
  
  3.  **Write the general solution:**
    The equation is $x_1 + 2x_2 + 3x_3 = 0$.
    $x_1 = -2x_2 - 3x_3$.
    $x_2$ and $x_3$ are free variables.
    $\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -2x_2 - 3x_3 \\ x_2 \\ x_3 \end{bmatrix} = x_2 \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + x_3 \begin{bmatrix} -3 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$
  
  4.  **Identify the basis:** The eigenspace is spanned by the vectors $\begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$ and $\begin{bmatrix} -3 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$. These vectors are linearly independent.
  
    Basis for the eigenspace corresponding to $\lambda = 3$: $\left\{ \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} -3 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \right\}$
### Eigenvalues of a Triangular Matrix
  
  The eigenvalues of a triangular matrix are the entries on its main diagonal.
### Example 4
  
  Find the eigenvalues of the matrix $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 0 & 0 & 4 \\ 0 & 0 & 5 \end{bmatrix}$.
  
  **Solution:**
  The matrix $A$ is upper triangular. The eigenvalues are the entries on the main diagonal.
  The eigenvalues are $\lambda_1 = 1$, $\lambda_2 = 0$, and $\lambda_3 = 5$.
### Example 5
  
  Construct an example of a $2 \times 2$ matrix with only one distinct eigenvalue.
  
  **Solution:**
  
  The eigenvalues of a triangular matrix are its diagonal entries. If we want only one distinct eigenvalue, we can make the diagonal entries the same.
  
  *   **Diagonal Example:** $A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$. The only eigenvalue is $\lambda = 3$.
  *   **Triangular (non-diagonal) Example:** $A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$. The only eigenvalue is $\lambda = 3$.
### Example 6
  
  Let $\lambda$ be an eigenvalue of an invertible matrix $A$. Show that $\lambda^{-1}$ is an eigenvalue of $A^{-1}$.
  
  **Solution:**
  
  Since $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue of $A$, there exists a nonzero eigenvector $\mathbf{x}$ such that $A\mathbf{x} = \lambda \mathbf{x}$.
  
  Since $A$ is invertible, $A^{-1}$ exists. Multiply both sides of the equation by $A^{-1}$ on the left:
  
  $A^{-1}(A\mathbf{x}) = A^{-1}(\lambda \mathbf{x})$
  $(A^{-1}A)\mathbf{x} = \lambda (A^{-1}\mathbf{x})$  (Scalar multiplication commutes)
  $I\mathbf{x} = \lambda (A^{-1}\mathbf{x})$
  $\mathbf{x} = \lambda (A^{-1}\mathbf{x})$
  
  Since $A$ is invertible, its eigenvalues must be nonzero (by the Invertible Matrix Theorem extension). So, $\lambda \neq 0$. We can divide by $\lambda$:
  
  $\frac{1}{\lambda}\mathbf{x} = A^{-1}\mathbf{x}$
  $\lambda^{-1}\mathbf{x} = A^{-1}\mathbf{x}$
  
  This shows that $A^{-1}\mathbf{x} = \lambda^{-1}\mathbf{x}$. Since $\mathbf{x}$ is a nonzero vector (it was an eigenvector of $A$), this means $\lambda^{-1}$ is an eigenvalue of $A^{-1}$ with the same corresponding eigenvector $\mathbf{x}$.
### The Invertible Matrix Theorem
  
  Let $A$ be a square $n \times n$ matrix. Then the following statements are equivalent.
  
  a. $A$ is an invertible matrix.
  ...
  s. The number 0 is not an eigenvalue of $A$.
### Theorem
  
  If $\mathbf{v}_1, ..., \mathbf{v}_r$ are eigenvectors that correspond to distinct eigenvalues $\lambda_1, ..., \lambda_r$ of an $n \times n$ matrix $A$, then the set $\{\mathbf{v}_1, ..., \mathbf{v}_r\}$ is linearly independent.
### Example 7
  
  Prove the preceding theorem.
  
  **Solution:**
  We prove this using proof by contradiction.
  Assume the vectors are linearly dependent. Then there exists some value c such that:
  $c_1\mathbf{v_1} + c_2\mathbf{v_2} + ... + c_p\mathbf{v_p} = 0$ where p is the index of the first linearly dependent vector, and $c_p$ is non zero.
  
  $A(c_1\mathbf{v_1} + c_2\mathbf{v_2} + ... + c_p\mathbf{v_p}) = A0 = 0$
  $c_1A\mathbf{v_1} + c_2A\mathbf{v_2} + ... + c_pA\mathbf{v_p} = 0$
  $c_1\lambda_1\mathbf{v_1} + c_2\lambda_2\mathbf{v_2} + ... + c_p\lambda_p\mathbf{v_p} = 0$ (Equation 2)
  
  Now, multiply the first equation by $\lambda_p$
  $\lambda_p c_1\mathbf{v_1} + \lambda_p c_2\mathbf{v_2} + ... + \lambda_p c_p\mathbf{v_p} = 0$ (Equation 1)
  
  Subtract equation 2 from equation 1
  $c_1(\lambda_p - \lambda_1)\mathbf{v_1} + c_2(\lambda_p - \lambda_2)\mathbf{v_2} + ... + c_{p-1}(\lambda_p - \lambda_{p-1})\mathbf{v_{p-1}} = 0$
  
  Since v1, v2, ..., vp-1 must be linearly independent, and all eigenvalues are distinct, then all the coefficients $c_1...c_{p-1}$ must be 0.
  This means that $c_p\mathbf{v_p} = 0$. Since $\mathbf{v_p}$ cannot be zero, this means that $c_p$ must be zero.
  This means all coefficients must be zero, which contradicts the initial assumption that the vectors are linearly dependent. Thus, the vectors must be linearly independent.
