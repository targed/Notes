## Section 4.1: Vector Spaces and Subspaces
### Vector Spaces
  
  A **vector space** is a nonempty set $V$ of objects (called **vectors**) on which are defined two operations, addition and scalar multiplication, subject to the following ten axioms.
  
  The axioms must hold for all vectors $\mathbf{u}$, $\mathbf{v}$, and $\mathbf{w}$ in $V$ and for all scalars $c$ and $d$. (Unless otherwise indicated, we assume in this chapter that scalars are real numbers).
### Vector Space Axioms
  
  1.  **Closure under addition:** The sum of $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$, denoted by $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}$, is in $V$.
  2.  **Commutativity of addition:** $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v} + \mathbf{u}$
  3.  **Associativity of addition:** $(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) + \mathbf{w} = \mathbf{u} + (\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{w})$
  4.  **Additive identity:** There is a zero vector $\mathbf{0}$ in $V$ such that $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{0} = \mathbf{u}$
  5.  **Additive inverse:** For each $\mathbf{u}$ in $V$, there is a vector $-\mathbf{u}$ in $V$ such that $\mathbf{u} + (-\mathbf{u}) = \mathbf{0}$
  6.  **Closure under scalar multiplication:** The scalar multiple of $\mathbf{u}$ by $c$, denoted by $c\mathbf{u}$, is in $V$.
  7.  **Distributivity:** $c(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) = c\mathbf{u} + c\mathbf{v}$
  8.  **Distributivity:** $(c + d)\mathbf{u} = c\mathbf{u} + d\mathbf{u}$
  9.  **Associativity of scalar multiplication:** $c(d\mathbf{u}) = (cd)\mathbf{u}$
  10. **Scalar multiplication identity:** $1\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{u}$
### Examples of Vector Spaces
  
  *   $\mathbb{R}^n$, $n \geq 1$
  *   $\mathbb{P}_n$, $n \geq 0$ ($\mathbb{P}_n$ is the set of polynomials of degree at most $n$)
### Example 1
  
  Let $V$ be the first quadrant in the $xy$-plane.
  
  $V = \left\{ \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix} : x \geq 0, y \geq 0 \right\}$
  
  a. If $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ are in $V$, is $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}$ in $V$?
  b. If $\mathbf{u}$ is in $V$ and $c$ is a scalar, is $c\mathbf{u}$ in $V$?
  
  **Solution:**
  
  a. Let $\mathbf{u} = \begin{bmatrix} u_1 \\ u_2 \end{bmatrix}$ and $\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \end{bmatrix}$ be in $V$.  This means $u_1 \geq 0$, $u_2 \geq 0$, $v_1 \geq 0$, and $v_2 \geq 0$.
  
  Then $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} u_1 + v_1 \\ u_2 + v_2 \end{bmatrix}$.
  
  Since $u_1 \geq 0$ and $v_1 \geq 0$, their sum $u_1 + v_1 \geq 0$.  Similarly, $u_2 + v_2 \geq 0$.  Therefore, $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}$ is in $V$. So, $V$ *is* closed under addition.
  
  b. Let $\mathbf{u} = \begin{bmatrix} u_1 \\ u_2 \end{bmatrix}$ be in $V$, so $u_1 \geq 0$ and $u_2 \geq 0$.  Let $c$ be a scalar.
  
  Then $c\mathbf{u} = \begin{bmatrix} cu_1 \\ cu_2 \end{bmatrix}$.
  
  If $c \geq 0$, then $cu_1 \geq 0$ and $cu_2 \geq 0$, so $c\mathbf{u}$ is in $V$.  However, if $c < 0$, then $cu_1 \leq 0$ and $cu_2 \leq 0$, so $c\mathbf{u}$ is *not* in $V$. Therefore, it is not true for *all* scalars, so $V$ is *not* closed under scalar multiplication, and $V$ is not a vector space.
### Example 2
  
  Show that a line which does not pass through the origin in $\mathbb{R}^2$ is not a vector space.
  
  Is a line through the origin in $\mathbb{R}^2$ a vector space?
  
  **Solution:**
  A line that does *not* go through the origin *does not* include the 0 vector. It therefore does not satisfy the axiom that requires a 0 vector to be in V.
  
  A line through the origin *can* be a vector space.
  
  Let's test the properties of a subspace to find out.
  Let $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ lie on the line. We can represent them as scaled versions of some vector $\mathbf{x}$
  $\mathbf{u} = a\mathbf{x}$
  $\mathbf{v} = b\mathbf{x}$
  $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} = a\mathbf{x} + b\mathbf{x} = (a+b)\mathbf{x}$
  This is still a multiple of $\mathbf{x}$, meaning it is still within the line, and thus, the first rule is satisfied.
  
  We know 0 lies within the line, so that axiom is also satisfied.
  
  Finally, $c\mathbf{u} = ca\mathbf{x}$. This is also a multiple of $\mathbf{x}$ and therefore is within the line.
  
  Because of this, a line through the origin *is* a vector space.
### Subspaces
  
  A **subspace** of a vector space $V$ is a subset $H$ of $V$ that has the following three properties:
  
  a. The zero vector of $V$ is in $H$.
  b. $H$ is closed under vector addition. That is, for all $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ in $H$, $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}$ is in $H$.
  c. $H$ is closed under scalar multiplication. That is, for all $\mathbf{u}$ in $H$ and all scalars $c$, $c\mathbf{u}$ is in $H$.
  
  Since all elements in $H$ are also in $V$, all of the remaining vector space axioms are also satisfied. Thus, $H$ is itself a vector space.
  
  It is generally much easier to prove that a given set forms a vector space by showing it is a subspace of a known vector space than by starting from scratch.
### Example 2 (continued)
  
  Show that a line through the origin in $\mathbb{R}^2$ is a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^2$ (and is thus a vector space).
  
  **Solution:**
  
  A line through the origin in $\mathbb{R}^2$ can be represented as $\text{Span}\{\mathbf{v}\}$ for some nonzero vector $\mathbf{v}$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$.  Let $H = \text{Span}\{\mathbf{v}\}$.  We need to check the three subspace properties:
  
  a. The zero vector is in $H$ because $0\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$.
  
  b. Let $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{w}$ be in $H$.  Then $\mathbf{u} = c\mathbf{v}$ and $\mathbf{w} = d\mathbf{v}$ for some scalars $c$ and $d$.  Then $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{w} = c\mathbf{v} + d\mathbf{v} = (c+d)\mathbf{v}$.  Since $(c+d)$ is a scalar, $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{w}$ is a scalar multiple of $\mathbf{v}$ and is therefore in $H$.  So $H$ is closed under addition.
  
  c. Let $\mathbf{u}$ be in $H$, and let $k$ be a scalar.  Then $\mathbf{u} = c\mathbf{v}$ for some scalar $c$.  Then $k\mathbf{u} = k(c\mathbf{v}) = (kc)\mathbf{v}$. Since $(kc)$ is a scalar, $k\mathbf{u}$ is a scalar multiple of $\mathbf{v}$ and is therefore in $H$.  So $H$ is closed under scalar multiplication.
  
  Since all three properties hold, $H$ is a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^2$.
### Example 3
  
  Show that $\mathbb{R}^2$ is not a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^3$.
  
  Then, show that $H = \left\{ \begin{bmatrix} s \\ t \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} : s, t \text{ real} \right\}$ is a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^3$.
  
  **Solution:**
  For R2 to be a subspace, R2 would need to be *within* R3, which is impossible.
  
  We check the three subspace properties for H:
  
  a. The zero vector of $\mathbb{R}^3$ is $\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$, which is in $H$ (take $s = 0$ and $t = 0$).
  
  b. Let $\mathbf{u} = \begin{bmatrix} s_1 \\ t_1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$ and $\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} s_2 \\ t_2 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$ be in $H$.  Then $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} s_1 + s_2 \\ t_1 + t_2 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$.  Since the third component is 0, $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}$ is in $H$.
  
  c. Let $\mathbf{u} = \begin{bmatrix} s \\ t \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$ be in $H$, and let $c$ be a scalar.  Then $c\mathbf{u} = \begin{bmatrix} cs \\ ct \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$. Since the third component is 0, $c\mathbf{u}$ is in $H$.
  
  Since all three properties hold, $H$ is a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^3$.
### Example 4
  
  Determine whether the set is a subspace of $\mathbb{P}_n$ for an appropriate value of $n$.
  
  All polynomials of the form $\mathbf{p}(t) = a + t^2$, where $a$ is in $\mathbb{R}$.
  
  **Solution:**
  The zero vector in $\mathbb{P}_n$ should be in our set, but it isn't. It should be a zero *polynomial*, which means *every* term is zero. But the vector $\mathbf{p}(t) = a + t^2$ has t^2, which means that t^2 is not zero.
  
  Therefore, it is not a subspace.
### Example 5
  
  Given two vectors $\mathbf{v}_1$ and $\mathbf{v}_2$ in a vector space $V$, show that $H = \text{Span}\{\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2\}$ is a subspace of $V$.
  
  **Solution:**
  
  We check the three subspace properties:
  
  a. The zero vector is in $H$ because $0\mathbf{v}_1 + 0\mathbf{v}_2 = \mathbf{0}$.
  
  b. Let $\mathbf{u} = a\mathbf{v}_1 + b\mathbf{v}_2$ and $\mathbf{w} = c\mathbf{v}_1 + d\mathbf{v}_2$ be in $H$.  Then $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{w} = (a+c)\mathbf{v}_1 + (b+d)\mathbf{v}_2$, which is a linear combination of $\mathbf{v}_1$ and $\mathbf{v}_2$, and is therefore in $H$.
  
  c. Let $\mathbf{u} = a\mathbf{v}_1 + b\mathbf{v}_2$ be in $H$, and let $k$ be a scalar.  Then $k\mathbf{u} = k(a\mathbf{v}_1 + b\mathbf{v}_2) = (ka)\mathbf{v}_1 + (kb)\mathbf{v}_2$, which is a linear combination of $\mathbf{v}_1$ and $\mathbf{v}_2$, and is therefore in $H$.
  
  Since all three properties hold, $H$ is a subspace of $V$.
### Subspaces and Span
  
  If $\mathbf{v}_1, ..., \mathbf{v}_p$ are in a vector space $V$, then $\text{Span}\{\mathbf{v}_1, ..., \mathbf{v}_p\}$ is a subspace of $V$.
  
  $\text{Span}\{\mathbf{v}_1, ..., \mathbf{v}_p\}$ is the subspace **spanned** by $\{\mathbf{v}_1, ..., \mathbf{v}_p\}$.
  
  Given any subspace $H$ of $V$, a **spanning set** for $H$ is a set $\{\mathbf{v}_1, ..., \mathbf{v}_p\}$ such that $H = \text{Span}\{\mathbf{v}_1, ..., \mathbf{v}_p\}$.
### Example 6
  
  Let $H$ be the set of all vectors of the form $\begin{bmatrix} 2t \\ 0 \\ -t \end{bmatrix}$. Show that $H$ is a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^3$.
  
  **Solution:**
  
  Notice that any vector in $H$ can be written as $t \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}$.  Therefore, $H = \text{Span} \left\{ \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} \right\}$.  Since the span of a set of vectors is always a subspace, $H$ is a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^3$.
### Example 7
  
  Let $W$ be the set of all vectors of the form shown, where $a$, $b$, and $c$ represent arbitrary real numbers. In each case, determine whether or not $W$ is a vector space.
  
  $\begin{bmatrix} 3a + b \\ 4 \\ a - 5b \end{bmatrix}$, $\begin{bmatrix} 4a + 3b \\ 0 \\ a + b + c \\ c - 2a \end{bmatrix}$
  
  **Solution:**
  
  *  First Set: $\begin{bmatrix} 3a + b \\ 4 \\ a - 5b \end{bmatrix}$
    Here the set does not contain a zero vector because the middle term is always going to be a 4. This fails the first axiom for vector spaces.
  * Second set:
  $\begin{bmatrix} 4a + 3b \\ 0 \\ a + b + c \\ c - 2a \end{bmatrix}$.  We try to write the vectors as a linear combination.
  
    $\begin{bmatrix} 4a + 3b \\ 0 \\ a + b + c \\ c - 2a \end{bmatrix} = a\begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 0 \\ 1 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix} + b\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + c\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$
    This shows that W is a subspace, since its a span of vectors. Therefore, it is a vector space.
### Example 8
  
  Prove that the zero vector in a vector space is unique.
  
  **Solution:**
  Suppose we have two zero vectors, $\mathbf{0_1}$ and $\mathbf{0_2}$. Since  $\mathbf{0_1}$ is a zero vector, $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{0_1} = \mathbf{u}$ for all $\mathbf{u}$.  Since $\mathbf{0_2}$ is a zero vector, $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{0_2} = \mathbf{u}$ for all $\mathbf{u}$.
  In particular, using $\mathbf{0_2}$ for u in the first equation gives $\mathbf{0_2} + \mathbf{0_1} = \mathbf{0_2}$. In particular, using $\mathbf{0_1}$ for u in the second equation gives $\mathbf{0_1} + \mathbf{0_2} = \mathbf{0_1}$.
  By commutivity: $\mathbf{0_2} + \mathbf{0_1} = \mathbf{0_1} + \mathbf{0_2}$
  
  This implies $\mathbf{0_1 = 0_2}$. Therefore, the zero vector must be unique.
### Example 9
  
  For what value(s) of $h$ will $\mathbf{y}$ be in the subspace of $\mathbb{R}^3$ spanned by $\mathbf{v}_1$, $\mathbf{v}_2$, and $\mathbf{v}_3$?
  
  $\mathbf{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix}$, $\mathbf{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ -4 \\ -7 \end{bmatrix}$, $\mathbf{v}_3 = \begin{bmatrix} -3 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$, $\mathbf{y} = \begin{bmatrix} -4 \\ 3 \\ h \end{bmatrix}$
  
  **Solution:**
  
  $\mathbf{y}$ is in the subspace spanned by $\mathbf{v}_1$, $\mathbf{v}_2$, and $\mathbf{v}_3$ if and only if the equation $c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2\mathbf{v}_2 + c_3\mathbf{v}_3 = \mathbf{y}$ has a solution.  This is equivalent to asking if the following system is consistent:
  
  $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 5 & -3 & -4 \\ -1 & -4 & 1 & 3 \\ -2 & -7 & 0 & h \end{bmatrix}$
  
  Row reduce:
  
  $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 5 & -3 & -4 \\ -1 & -4 & 1 & 3 \\ -2 & -7 & 0 & h \end{bmatrix} \sim \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 5 & -3 & -4 \\ 0 & 1 & -2 & -1 \\ 0 & 3 & -6 & h-8 \end{bmatrix} \sim \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 5 & -3 & -4 \\ 0 & 1 & -2 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & h-5 \end{bmatrix}$
  
  The system is consistent if and only if $h - 5 = 0$, or $h = 5$.
