- **1. Motivation (from Sipser)**
  
  *   When working with Context-Free Grammars (CFGs), it's often useful to have them in a **simplified or standardized format**.
  *   Standard forms help in designing algorithms for CFLs (like parsing or deciding membership) and simplifying theoretical proofs.
  *   Chomsky Normal Form (CNF) is one of the simplest and most widely used normal forms for CFGs.
  ---
- **2. Definition (Definition 6, Sunitha)**
  
  *   A CFG $G=(V, T, P, S)$ is said to be in **Chomsky Normal Form (CNF)** if *every* production rule in $P$ takes one of the following two forms:
    1.  $A \to BC$ where $A, B, C \in V$ ($B$ and $C$ must be variables, not terminals).
        *(Rule: A variable derives exactly two variables).*
    2.  $A \to a$ where $A \in V$ and $a \in T$.
        *(Rule: A variable derives exactly one terminal).*
  
  *   **Exception for $\epsilon$:** If the language $L(G)$ contains the empty string $\epsilon$, then the rule $S \to \epsilon$ is allowed, where $S$ is the start symbol. However, if this rule is present, $S$ must *not* appear on the right-hand side of *any* production in the grammar. (This is often handled by starting the conversion with a new start symbol $S_0 \to S$ if $L(G)$ contains $\epsilon$).
  ---
- **3. Restrictions Imposed by CNF**
  
  *   CNF strictly limits the structure of the right-hand side (RHS) of productions:
    *   The RHS must consist of either exactly two variables or exactly one terminal.
    *   No mixing of terminals and variables on the RHS.
    *   No unit productions (like $A \to B$).
    *   No $\epsilon$-productions (except possibly $S \to \epsilon$ under the special condition above).
  ---
- **4. Conversion to CNF (Theorem 2.9 Sipser, Procedure Sunitha)**
  
  *   **Theorem:** Any context-free language $L$ can be generated by a CFG in Chomsky Normal Form. (More precisely, any CFL $L$ not containing $\epsilon$ has a CNF grammar. If $L$ contains $\epsilon$, $L \setminus \{\epsilon\}$ has a CNF grammar, and $S \to \epsilon$ can be added carefully).
  *   **Conversion Procedure:** Given an arbitrary CFG $G$, we can convert it to an equivalent CNF grammar $G_{CNF}$ using the following steps:
  
    *   **(Step 0: Handle $\epsilon$ in $L(G)$ - Implicit/Optional First Step)**
        *   If the language contains $\epsilon$, we typically first ensure the start symbol $S$ does not appear on the RHS of any rule. If it does, create a new start symbol $S_0$ and add the rule $S_0 \to S$. $S_0$ becomes the new start symbol. Add $S_0 \to \epsilon$ if needed. The rest of the conversion applies to the rules of the original $S$ and other non-terminals. (This ensures the $S \to \epsilon$ exception rule for CNF is handled correctly).
  
    *   **Step 1: Eliminate $\epsilon$-Productions (Null Productions)**
        *   Remove all rules of the form $A \to \epsilon$.
        *   For every rule $B \to \dots A \dots$, where $A$ is nullable (i.e., $A \Rightarrow^* \epsilon$), add a new rule $B \to \dots \dots$ where the $A$ is omitted. If the RHS contains multiple nullable variables, add rules for all combinations of omitting them. (See Grammar Simplification notes for details).
  
    *   **Step 2: Eliminate Unit Productions**
        *   Remove all rules of the form $A \to B$, where $A, B \in V$.
        *   For every unit rule $A \to B$, find all *non-unit* productions starting from $B$, say $B \to \gamma$. Add a new rule $A \to \gamma$ to the grammar. Repeat until no unit rules remain (handle chains like $A \to B \to C$). (See Grammar Simplification notes).
  
    *   **Step 3: Convert Remaining Productions to CNF Form**
        *   At this point, all rules are of the form $A \to \gamma$ where $\gamma$ contains only terminals, or $\gamma$ contains a mix of terminals and variables, or $\gamma$ contains only variables, and $|\gamma| \ge 1$.
        *   **Handle Terminals:**
            *   If a rule is $A \to a$ (already CNF), keep it.
            *   If a rule has a terminal $a$ appearing on the RHS alongside other symbols (terminals or variables), e.g., $A \to aB$ or $A \to BCd$ or $A \to abc$:
                *   Introduce a *new* dedicated variable $C_a$ for each such terminal $a$.
                *   Add the production $C_a \to a$ (which is in CNF).
                *   Replace the terminal $a$ in the original rule(s) with the new variable $C_a$. (e.g., $A \to aB$ becomes $A \to C_a B$; $A \to BCd$ becomes $A \to B C C_d$; $A \to abc$ becomes $A \to C_a C_b C_c$).
        *   **Handle RHS with > 2 Variables:**
            *   If a rule is now $A \to BC$ (already CNF), keep it.
            *   If a rule has more than two variables on the RHS, e.g., $A \to BCD$:
                *   Introduce a *new* intermediate variable, say $D_1$.
                *   Replace the rule with two new rules: $A \to B D_1$ and $D_1 \to CD$. Both are now in CNF.
            *   If the rule is $A \to BCDE$:
                *   Introduce $D_1 \to CD$. Replace: $A \to B D_1 E$.
                *   Introduce $D_2 \to D_1 E$. Replace: $A \to B D_2$.
                *   Add rules: $A \to B D_2$, $D_2 \to D_1 E$, $D_1 \to CD$. All are in CNF. (Chain the variables pairwise).
  ---
- **5. Examples**
  
  *   **Example 4.33:** Convert $G = (\{S, A, B\}, \{a, b\}, P, S)$ where $P=\{S \to bA \mid aB, A \to bAA \mid aS \mid a, B \to aBB \mid bS \mid b\}$.
    *   No $\epsilon$ or unit productions exist.
    *   **Step 3a (Handle Terminals):**
        *   Keep $A \to a$, $B \to b$.
        *   Introduce $C_a \to a$, $C_b \to b$.
        *   Replace terminals in other rules:
            *   $S \to C_b A \mid C_a B$
            *   $A \to C_b AA \mid C_a S \mid a$ (keep $A \to a$)
            *   $B \to C_a BB \mid C_b S \mid b$ (keep $B \to b$)
        *   Grammar now: $S \to C_b A \mid C_a B$, $A \to C_b AA \mid C_a S \mid a$, $B \to C_a BB \mid C_b S \mid b$, $C_a \to a$, $C_b \to b$.
    *   **Step 3b (Handle RHS > 2 Variables):**
        *   $A \to C_b AA$: Introduce $D \to AA$. Replace with $A \to C_b D$. Add $D \to AA$.
        *   $B \to C_a BB$: Introduce $E \to BB$. Replace with $B \to C_a E$. Add $E \to BB$.
    *   **Final CNF Grammar:**
        *   $S \to C_b A \mid C_a B$
        *   $A \to C_b D \mid C_a S \mid a$
        *   $B \to C_a E \mid C_b S \mid b$
        *   $C_a \to a$
        *   $C_b \to b$
        *   $D \to AA$
        *   $E \to BB$
  
  *   **Example 4.34:** Convert $G=(\{S, A, B\}, \{a, b\}, P, S)$ where $P = \{S \to AB \mid aB, A \to aab \mid \epsilon, B \to bbA\}$.
    *   **Step 1 (Eliminate $\epsilon$):** $A$ is nullable. $S \to AB$ yields $S \to B$. $B \to bbA$ yields $B \to bb$. Remove $A \to \epsilon$.
        *   Intermediate grammar: $S \to AB \mid B \mid aB$, $A \to aab$, $B \to bbA \mid bb$.
    *   **Step 2 (Eliminate Unit):** $S \to B$. Replace with $S \to bbA \mid bb$.
        *   Intermediate grammar: $S \to AB \mid bbA \mid aB \mid bb$, $A \to aab$, $B \to bbA \mid bb$.
    *   **Step 3a (Handle Terminals):** Introduce $C_a \to a, C_b \to b$.
        *   $S \to AB \mid C_b C_b A \mid C_a B \mid C_b C_b$
        *   $A \to C_a C_a C_b$
        *   $B \to C_b C_b A \mid C_b C_b$
        *   $C_a \to a$
        *   $C_b \to b$
    *   **Step 3b (Handle RHS > 2 Variables):**
        *   $S \to C_b C_b A$: Replace with $S \to C_b D$, Add $D \to C_b A$.
        *   $A \to C_a C_a C_b$: Replace with $A \to C_a E$, Add $E \to C_a C_b$.
        *   $B \to C_b C_b A$: Replace with $B \to C_b D$.
        *   ($S \to C_b C_b$ and $B \to C_b C_b$ need intermediate state too): Add $F \to C_b C_b$. Replace with $S \to F$ and $B \to F$. (Oops, unit rules $S \to F, B \to F$. Need to re-eliminate after this step, or better, use $F$ directly). Let's use $F$ directly.
    *   **Refined Step 3b & Final CNF:**
        *   Introduce $D \to C_b A$
        *   Introduce $E \to C_a C_b$
        *   Introduce $F \to C_b C_b$
        *   Final Rules:
            *   $S \to AB \mid C_b D \mid C_a B \mid F$
            *   $A \to C_a E$
            *   $B \to C_b D \mid F$
            *   $D \to C_b A$
            *   $E \to C_a C_b$
            *   $F \to C_b C_b$
            *   $C_a \to a$
            *   $C_b \to b$
  ---
- **6. Usefulness of CNF**
  
  *   **Parsing Algorithms:** CNF is required for some parsing algorithms like CYK (Cocke-Younger-Kasami), which determines if a string can be generated by a CFG in polynomial time ($O(n^3)$).
  *   **Simplicity:** The restricted format simplifies proofs and analysis of CFL properties.
