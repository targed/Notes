# Spaceship Earth
- The earth is a relatively small rocky planet
- Since the earth spins, it bulges at the equator
- Thus, the earth is not a sphere.
  - It’s mean radius is 6371 KM
  - Its equatorial radius is 6,378.137 KM
  - It’s polar radius is 6356.752 KM
# What does the universe look like from earth
- With the naked eye, we can see more than 2000 stars as well as the milky way
# Constellations
- A constellation is a region of the sky that humans have found patterns in
- Constellations are very prevent through human history. They date at least 17000 years ago
- Stars in constellations are NOT next to each other
- The constellations we see depend on the time of year
  - Earth's orbit changes the apparent location of the Sun among the stars.
  - As the Earth orbits the Sun, the Sun appears to move eastward along the ecliptic.
  - At midnight, the stars on our meridian are opposite the Sun in the sky.
# The celestial sphere
- A projection of the universe onto a sphere
  - We look at it from the inside
- Stars at different distances appear to lie on the celestial sphere
- The ecliptic is the suns apparent path through the celestial sphere
- Important points
  - The north celestial pole is directly above earths North Pole
  - The south celestial pole is directly above earths south poles
  - The celestial equator is a projection of earths equator onto the sky
# The local sky
- An objects altitude (above horizon) and direction (along horizon) specify its location in the sky
  - Using this system, you can identify any point in the sky
- Important points
  - Meridian: line passing through zenith and connecting N and S points in the horizon
  - Zenith: the point directly overhead
  - Horizon: all points 90 degrees away from the zenith
# Why do stars rise and set
- Earth rotates from west to east so stars appear to circle from east to west
- The synodic rotation period is the time it takes the sun to go from noon to noon (1 day)
- The sidereal rotation period is shorter than 24 hours because it is the time a star appear in the same place in the sky. This is due to the earth orbiting the sun. (23H 56M 4S)
  - This is why we have a leap year
# Our view from earth
- Stars near the North Pole appear to never set
- We cannot see the stars near the South Pole
- The sky varies with latitude.
  - The observers position on earth determines which constellations remain below the horizon
- Your altitude of the celestial pole is equal to your latitude on the earth
# Complications
- Constipations and orbital positions are hard to keep consistent because the earth is also revolving around the sun
- The earths orbit around the sun is not perfectly circular, it is eliptical
  - Its closest point to the sun is called the perihelion
  - It’s furthest point from the sun is called the aphelion
- The sidereal orbital period of the earth around the sun is the time it takes the earth to orbit once around the sun. 365.25 days

# Seasons
- The suns altitude in the sky at the same time every day changes throughout the year
- Earth's axis points in the same direction (to Polaris) all year round, so its orientation relative to the Sun changes as Earth orbits the Sun.
- Summer occurs in your hemisphere when sunlight hits it more directly; winter occurs when the sunlight is less direct.
- Axis tilt is the key to the seasons; without it, we would not have seasons on Earth.
- Although the axis seems fixed on human time scales, it actually precesses over about 26,000 years.
  - Polaris won't always be the North Star.
  - Positions of equinoxes shift around orbit; e.g., spring equinox, once in Aries, is now in Pisces!
- Why doesn't distance matter?
  - Variation of Earth–Sun distance is small—about 3%; this small variation is overwhelmed by the effects of axis tilt.
  - Variation in any season of each hemisphere—Sun distance is even smaller!
# How do we mark the progression of the seasons?
- We define four special points:
  - Northern summer (June) solstice
    - Northern summer (June) solstice: highest path; rise and set at most extreme north of due east
  - Northern winter (December) solstice
    - Northern winter (December) solstice: lowest path; rise and set at most extreme south of due east
  - Northern spring (March) equinox
  - Northern fall (September) equinox
- Equinoxes: Sun rises precisely due east and sets precisely due west.
# Recap
- What causes the seasons?
  - The tilt of the Earth's axis causes sunlight to hit different parts of the Earth more directly during the summer and less directly during the winter.
  - We can specify the position of an object in the local sky by its altitude above the horizon and its direction along the horizon.
  - The summer and winter solstices are when the Northern Hemisphere gets its most and least direct sunlight, respectively. The spring and fall equinoxes are when both hemispheres get equally direct sunlight.
- How does the orientation of Earth's axis change with time?
  - The tilt remains about 23.5o (so the season pattern is not affected), but Earth has a 26,000 year precession cycle that slowly and subtly changes the orientation of Earth's axis.
