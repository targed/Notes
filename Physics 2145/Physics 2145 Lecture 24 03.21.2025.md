# Lecture 24: Magnetic Field
  
  This lecture introduces the concept of magnetism, magnetic fields, and how they are produced by both permanent magnets and electric currents.
## Permanent Magnets
  
  *   **North and South Poles:** Every magnet has two poles, designated as North (N) and South (S).
  *   **Interaction:**
    *   Like poles repel each other (N-N, S-S).
    *   Opposite poles attract each other (N-S).
  *   **No Magnetic Monopoles:** Unlike electric charges (which can exist as isolated positive or negative charges), magnetic poles always come in pairs. If you break a magnet in half, each piece will have its own North and South pole. Isolated magnetic poles (monopoles) have not been observed to exist.
  *   **Earth's Magnetic Field:**
    *   The Earth acts like a giant magnet.
    *   **Convention:** The pole of a compass needle (which is a small magnet) that is attracted to the Earth's geographic North Pole is defined as the magnet's *North* pole.
    *   This means the Earth's geographic North Pole is actually near the Earth's *magnetic South* pole. So, Santa Claus lives near the Earth's magnetic South Pole!
## The Magnetic Field (B)
  
  *   **Concept:** Every magnet creates a *magnetic field* in the space surrounding it.
  *   **Effect:** If another magnet (like a compass needle) is placed within this field, it experiences a force or torque.
  *   **Direction:** The direction of the magnetic field (**B**) at any point is defined as the direction that the North pole of a compass needle would point if placed at that location.
  *   **Strength:** The strength of the magnetic field is related to the magnitude of the force or torque exerted on another magnet. Stronger fields exert stronger forces/torques.
  *   **Units:** The SI unit for magnetic field strength is the **Tesla (T)**.
  *   **Magnetic Field Lines:** Similar to electric field lines, magnetic field lines are used to visualize the magnetic field:
    *   Lines point *away* from North poles and *towards* South poles outside the magnet.
    *   Lines form *closed loops*, continuing through the magnet from South to North.
    *   The density of lines indicates the field strength (closer lines = stronger field).
    *   Lines never cross.
  * **Visualization**: Iron filings can be used to map out the pattern of magnetic field lines around a magnet.
## Electric Currents Also Create Magnetic Fields
  
  A key discovery in physics (by Oersted) was that electric currents produce magnetic fields. This unified electricity and magnetism. We will look at the fields created by three common current configurations:
  
  1.  A long, straight wire carrying current.
  2.  A circular loop of wire carrying current.
  3.  A solenoid (a coil of wire).
## Magnetic Field of a Long Straight Wire
  
  *   **Magnitude:** The magnetic field strength (B) at a perpendicular distance *r* from a long, straight wire carrying current *I* is given by:
  
    $B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}$
  
    Where:
    *   $B$ is the magnetic field strength in Tesla (T).
    *   $I$ is the current in Amperes (A).
    *   $r$ is the perpendicular distance from the wire in meters (m).
    *   $\mu_0$ is the **permeability of free space**, a fundamental constant:
        $\mu_0 = 4\pi \times 10^{-7} \text{ T}\cdot\text{m/A}$
  
  *   **Direction: Right-Hand Rule 1**
    1.  Point the thumb of your right hand in the direction of the conventional current (I).
    2.  Your fingers will curl around the wire in the direction of the magnetic field lines. The magnetic field lines form concentric circles around the wire.
## Magnetic Field at the Center of a Current Loop
  
  *   **Magnitude (at the center of a single loop):** The magnetic field strength (B) at the exact center of a circular loop of radius *R* carrying current *I* is:
  
    $B_{center} = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2R}$
  
  *   **Magnitude (at the center of N loops):** If the loop consists of *N* tightly wound turns:
  
    $B_{center} = \frac{\mu_0 N I}{2R}$
  
  *   **Direction: Right-Hand Rule 2 (for loops/solenoids)**
    1.  Curl the fingers of your right hand in the direction of the conventional current (I) around the loop.
    2.  Your thumb will point in the direction of the magnetic field *at the center* of the loop.
  * **Field is not Uniform:** The magnetic field is strongest at the center and weakens as you move away. The formula above only applies to the center point.
## Magnetic Field of a Long Solenoid
  
  *   **Definition:** A solenoid is a coil of wire, often wound in the shape of a helix. A "long" solenoid is one whose length is much greater than its radius.
  *   **Field Inside:** The magnetic field inside a long, ideal solenoid is:
    *   **Uniform:** It has nearly the same strength and direction at all points well within the solenoid.
    *   **Strong:** Much stronger than the field outside.
    *   Directed along the axis of the solenoid.
  *   **Magnitude (inside):**
  
    $B = \mu_0 n I$
  
    Where:
    *   $B$ is the magnetic field strength inside the solenoid (T).
    *   $\mu_0$ is the permeability of free space.
    *   $n$ is the **number of turns per unit length** ($n = N/L$, where N is the total number of turns and L is the length of the solenoid). Units: turns/meter.
    *   $I$ is the current in the wire (A).
  
  *   **Field Outside:** The magnetic field outside a long, ideal solenoid is very weak, often approximated as zero.
  *   **Direction: Right-Hand Rule 2**
    1.  Curl the fingers of your right hand in the direction the current wraps around the solenoid.
    2.  Your thumb will point in the direction of the magnetic field *inside* the solenoid (along its axis).
## Summary
  
  This lecture introduced magnetic poles, the concept of the magnetic field (**B**, measured in Tesla), and how to visualize it using field lines. Importantly, it established that electric currents are a source of magnetic fields. We learned specific formulas and right-hand rules to determine the magnitude and direction of the magnetic field for three key configurations: a long straight wire, the center of a current loop, and the interior of a long solenoid.
